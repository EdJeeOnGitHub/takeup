\begin{comment}
    Moving services farther typically reduces take‑up of preventive care. Yet when take-up is observable, requiring greater effort can raise the social image return to participation, dampening the distance penalty. We study this interaction in a community deworming campaign across 144 Kenyan communities. We randomly vary distance to treatment sites—close or far—and cross‑randomize public signals that increase the observability of deworming. Reduced form results show that, without signals, moving from close to far lowers take‑up by 16 percentage points; with signals, the close--far gap is 7 percentage points smaller. We estimate a structural model that separates the private cost and social image channels and accounts for the fact that, absent signals, observability falls with distance. The estimates imply that, as distance increases and participation falls, being seen deworming becomes more informative about motivation. With signals, social image returns rise with distance and partly offset private costs, so take-up declines only 80–90\% as much as it would if social image played no role. Without signals, declining observability reduces social image returns and take-up declines 10–30\% more. These dynamics matter for optimal site placement: a policymaker who accounts for social image can achieve the same deworming coverage with 6\% fewer sites when actions are highly observable.\\

\section{Introduction}
\label{intro}



Governments routinely steer behavior by altering private costs. Yet behavior is also shaped by social image concerns. When actions are observable, cost changes affect take-up twice: directly, by changing private incentives, and indirectly, because shifts in take-up lead observers to update what participation implies about motivation, shifting the social image payoff. This feedback from take-up into beliefs can attenuate or amplify the direct cost response—a social multiplier in the sense of \citet{Benabou_laws_2011}.

Whether such forces operate in real-world settings, and how large they are, is an open empirical question with potential policy relevance. Consider moving a public health campaign closer to a community: lower travel costs draw in people who were previously deterred by distance. If the new participants are perceived as less motivated, the social image payoff to attendance may fall because observers infer that “it must not take much dedication to attend now”. At the other extreme, when services are placed very near—for example, a blood drive in one’s workplace—participation may become nearly universal, and abstention may carry sharper stigma. In both cases, changes in distance shift participation and therefore the inferences observers draw from take-up.%\footnote{Unlike work on crowding out from monetary incentives \citep[e.g.,][]{ariely2009}, where paying for an action can make it less informative about intrinsic or prosocial motivation and thereby reduce social image rewards, we study how non-monetary cost changes shift who participates, changing what participation implies about motivation.}

Despite the intuitive appeal of such multipliers, empirical evidence on whether they operate in the field—and, if so, how large they are—remains limited. We ask: When participation is socially visible, do distance changes generate social image multipliers in equilibrium? How large are these in practice? And what do they imply for the spatial allocation of treatment sites?

To answer these questions, we collaborate with the Government of Kenya on a community deworming program targeting 200{,}000 adults. Treatment is free and offered at centrally located sites. Deworming is widely viewed as a responsible health behavior in this setting: in baseline vignettes, 95 percent of adults report that they would praise someone who participates and 70 percent report that they would look down on someone who does not. Fewer than 2 percent associate deworming with sickness or poor hygiene.

We leverage the program’s geographic scope to implement a factorial design that randomizes both distance and observability at the community level. Distance provides a salient, common knowledge source of cost variation and, in an observable setting, can change both who participates and what participation implies about motivation. First, we randomly assign 144 communities to be Close (0–1.25 km) or Far (1.25–2.5 km) from their assigned treatment site, inducing an average 1 km difference in travel distance.\footnote{Sites are hosted at primary schools/churchers/mosques and only a limited number can open. Within this capacity constraint, we randomly assign communities to the Close or Far distance category.} Second, we vary observability with public signals provided upon deworming: a colorful bracelet or an indelible ink mark. The bracelet is highly visible and novel, whereas the ink mark is familiar from elections and is nearly costless to implement. We include two additional comparison arms: a pure control with no item and a low-visibility wall calendar incentive, which provides an item with limited scope for public display and helps distinguish signaling effects from generic incentive effects. 

Because distance affects both travel costs and, in the absence of public signals, how easily participation is observed, distance variation alone cannot isolate travel cost effects from social image effects. The signaling treatments provide the independent variation in observability needed to disentangle these channels. The signaling treatments may also operate through reminders or by providing information about peers’ take-up. To test these channels, we cross-randomize SMS messages that remind a random subsample of 2,635 adults about the campaign and report the current local take-up rate.

We first document how distance and signaling treatments affect observability. In Control, deworming is highly observable in Close communities: respondents report knowing 75 percent of peers’ deworming status. Observability declines with distance: knowledge is 12 percentage points lower in Far than in Close Control communities (\(p=0.056\)), consistent with learning through direct encounters at treatment sites or seeing others en route to treatment. The public signals largely offset the distance related decline: in Far communities, relative to Control, bracelets raise knowledge of peers’ status by 22 percentage points (\(p<0.001\)) and ink raises it by 16 percentage points (\(p=0.007\)), while in Close communities the same signals raise observability by only 3--5 percentage points (\(p=0.357\)--\(0.644\)).

We then examine take-up by directly monitoring 9{,}805 adults at treatment sites. Three reduced form findings emerge. First, in the absence of signals, a one-kilometer increase in distance lowers take-up by 16 percentage points (\(p<0.001\)). Survey beliefs track this pattern: respondents predict a 12 percentage point decline in deworming participation as distance increases ($p=0.002$). Second, bracelets increase take-up by 7.5 percentage points relative to control (\(p=0.008\)) and by 5 percentage points relative to the calendar (\(p=0.048\)), while ink has no detectable average effect on take-up (\(p=0.35\)).\footnote{In the structural estimation, the ink mark has negative private value on average, consistent with disutility from being marked. In addition, because indelible ink is commonly used in elections in Kenya, it may be perceived as a less specific signal of deworming participation.} The SMS treatments leave these patterns unchanged, ruling out reminders or social learning as stand alone drivers. Third, consistent with the fact that both signals raise observability much more in Far than in Close communities, the pooled effect of any public signal (bracelet or ink) relative to the low-visibility arms (Control/Calendar) is 6.8 percentage points larger in Far than in Close communities (\(p=0.083\)).

Two alternative explanations could generate stronger effects in Far communities without any role for social image. First, if more individuals are near the participation margin in Far communities, then any additive incentive should have a larger effect there. The calendar arm provides a placebo test: its take‑up effect is similar in Close and Far communities ($p = 0.959$), while the Close–Far interaction appears only for the public signals. Second, the bracelet could have higher private value for the marginal adult in Far communities and therefore compensate travel costs more effectively. We test this directly using willingness-to-pay (WTP) and endline gift choice measures. The WTP exercise indicates that the calendar is privately valued at least as highly as the bracelet, and gift choices elicited from adults who did not deworm (our most relevant proxy for marginal participants) provide no evidence that the bracelet’s private value is higher at farther distances. Finally, we formalize a benchmark model in which incentives operate only through private utility and show that it cannot jointly rationalize the stronger Far effects of the public signals and the near flat distance pattern for the calendar.

These reduced form patterns are consistent with a social image mechanism in which distance and public signals interact through observability and inference. However, taken alone they do not identify the social multiplier, because distance simultaneously changes travel costs and the equilibrium social image return by shifting who participates and, absent signals, how observable participation is. Quantifying the multiplier requires a counterfactual distance gradient: how strongly would take-up respond to distance if social image concerns were shut down? We therefore develop and estimate a structural model building on \citet{Benabou_laws_2011} in which individuals trade off private benefits net of travel costs against a social image payoff shaped by observability and belief updating. We define the social multiplier as the ratio (in magnitude) of the total distance effect on take-up to the direct travel cost effect that would obtain if social image incentives were shut down. The multiplier is below one (mitigation) when the image return rises with distance and offsets part of the cost increase, and above one (amplification) when the image return falls with distance and reinforces the cost effect. 

Identification combines three ingredients. First, the calendar arm together with WTP and gift choice measures provides evidence on the relative private value of the bracelet versus the calendar, allowing us to bound how much of the take-up difference between the bracelet and calendar arms could be attributed to private rewards. Second, the cross‑randomization of distance and observability, together with survey measures of beliefs about others’ deworming status, identifies how observable participation is in each arm and how observability changes with distance. Third, the distance‑by‑observability gradients in take‑up identify responsiveness to travel costs and image incentives and, combined with the first two ingredients, allow us to recover the social image return to being observed deworming and the implied social multiplier. 

The estimated model matches the reduced form evidence and separates the distance gradient into the part due to travel costs and the part due to endogenous changes in the image return. We provide a causal estimate of the implied social image multiplier. With public signals that keep participation highly observable across distance, the estimated multiplier lies between 0.8 and 0.9, implying mitigation. When observability instead declines with distance, the multiplier lies between 1.1 and 1.3, implying amplification. Decomposing the estimated multiplier shows that belief updating is quantitatively important even holding observability fixed. Under ink, mitigation is driven primarily by stronger inference about motivation, whereas under bracelets it reflects both stronger inference and sustained observability as distance increases. In Control and Calendar, inference shifts in the mitigating direction but is outweighed by the decline in observability, producing amplification.

These multipliers have first-order implications for spatial policy design. In the final part of the paper, we embed the estimated model in a site-allocation problem using geographic data on the 144 study communities and 1,451 feasible sites. Relative to a planner who calibrates demand at short distances and treats social image returns as distance-invariant, accounting for the multiplier increases the required number of sites from 100 to 107 when observability is allowed to decline with distance, but reduces the required number of sites from 96 to 90 when public signals sustain high observability. Taken together, the same deworming coverage target can require 107 sites in a low-observability environment versus 90 sites when observability is sustained---a difference of 16 percent.

While these magnitudes are context specific, they illustrate a general point: policies that affect observability can change not only the level of take-up but also the equilibrium feedback determining whether cost shocks are amplified or partially offset. In settings with higher travel costs or stronger social image concerns, ignoring this feedback could generate even larger distortions.

Our paper makes three contributions. First, to the best of our knowledge, we provide the first field experimental estimate of a social multiplier generated by social image concerns, and we quantify its magnitude in our context. By cross-randomizing travel distance and community-level observability, we study how a policy-relevant cost margin interacts with social image incentives. Existing field experiments identify whether social image incentives matter at a given margin, but typically do not show how cost-induced changes in participation feed back into the social image payoff itself \citep{bursztyn_social_2017,bursztyn_status_2018,chandrasekhar_social_2018,dellavigna_voting_2017,karing,breza19}. Our study is most closely related to \citet{karing}, who shows that attaching public signals to a more effortful childhood vaccination increases timely completion. Our key distinction is that we hold the action fixed and vary a cost margin (distance), allowing us to trace how incremental cost changes shift both participation and what participation implies about motivation---the equilibrium feedback captured by the social multiplier.

This contribution rests on three features of our design and data: (i) we orthogonally randomize distance and observability for the same preventive action, generating exogenous variation in both private costs and observability while holding the signal’s meaning fixed; (ii) we collect survey-based measures of observability and private valuations of the incentives to discipline the role of private rewards versus observability; and (iii) we use the experimental variation and auxiliary measures to estimate a structural social image model that recovers the multiplier. Related observational work studies how social norms shape responses to taxes, transfers, and regulation \citep[e.g.,][]{jia2019individual,besley2023norms,bobonis2025norms}, but typically cannot disentangle material incentives, observability, and beliefs without strong assumptions. Our experiment isolates all three.

Second, we contribute to work on contextual factors that shape social image incentives and public good provision \citep{kessler_announcements_2017,perez-truglia_partisan_2017,karing}. We complement \citet{ariely2009}, who show that image concerns interact with monetary incentives, by varying a non-monetary cost---distance. Distance shifts the composition of participants without introducing monetary rewards, so image inferences are not mechanically confounded with financial motives. More broadly, governments rarely pay people to adopt preventive health behaviors, but they routinely choose where to locate services. Our setting therefore highlights a policy-relevant cost margin through which image incentives can vary.

Third, we connect micro-level estimates of social image incentives to a concrete problem of spatial policy design, contributing to a growing literature that combines field experiments with structural modeling to inform counterfactual policy choices \citep{ToddWolpin,KaboskiTownsend,DufloHannaRyan,LagakosMobarakWaugh}. We embed the estimated social image model and multiplier in a site location problem, combining it with geocoded data on 144 communities and 1{,}451 feasible sites to choose the minimum number and placement of treatment sites needed to reach a coverage target. This exercise shows how a planner who ignores the endogenous response of social image returns to distance systematically misjudges how many sites are needed and where. More broadly, the framework illustrates how micro estimates of social image incentives can be brought to bear on facility location decisions, and shows that different underlying drivers of low take-up at longer distances---high travel costs versus low observability---have different implications for where and how many sites to open.

\section{The Setting} \label{setting}

%POSSIBLE REWRITE OF THIS SECTION WITHOUT SUBHEADINGS? i.e. more traditional approach

Intestinal worms impose a substantial health burden in many low-income settings. The World Health Organization estimates that roughly 24 percent of the world’s population is infected with soil-transmitted helminths \citeyearpar{who2023}, and severe infections can cause abdominal pain, anemia, and malnutrition \citep{hotez2008helminth}. While community-wide deworming can generate large social benefits by reducing reinfection, the private health gains for adults are often modest \citep{CHAN1997438,ahuja2015should}, making adult take-up a natural setting in which non-material motives such as social image may matter.

\paragraph{Deworming in Kenya.}
Deworming is an established preventive behavior in our study area. Since 2009, Kenya’s National School-Based Deworming Program has treated more than 5 million children in high-endemic areas, including our study counties \citep{givewell2017}. Consistent with this, in our baseline survey 78 percent of adults report being aware of deworming treatment and 49 percent know it should be taken every 3--12 months (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-bal}, Panel~B). When asked who is at risk of worm infection, 94 percent name children and 70 percent name adults.

\paragraph{Is adult deworming socially rewarded or stigmatized?}
A natural concern is that seeking deworming treatment could be embarrassing if it were perceived as revealing infection or poor hygiene. In our context, baseline responses indicate the opposite: 95 percent of adults say they would praise someone who takes deworming treatment, and 70 percent say they would look down on someone who does not (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-bal}, Panel~C).\footnote{These perceptions are elicited in hypothetical scenarios at baseline and may reflect some social desirability in reporting. We use them primarily to characterize pre-campaign attitudes. Our causal results rely on administratively measured take-up and randomized variation in distance and observability.} Figure~\ref{fig:praise-stigma-plot} shows that these judgments are comparable in magnitude to attitudes toward childhood immunization and the avoidance of open defecation. Importantly, fewer than 2 percent report worrying that taking deworming pills would signal dirtiness or illness (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-bal}, Panel~C). Taken together, these patterns suggest that the net social image return to being observed deworming is positive on average in our setting.

Open ended endline responses are consistent with this interpretation: respondents frequently describe deworming as reflecting health conscientiousness and responsibility (e.g., ``it shows I care about my health’’) and some also emphasize civic virtue and leadership, framing participation as setting an example for others (e.g.,``I must take it as a leader to set an example to others.’’).   

\paragraph{What does deworming signal?}
Social judgments about deworming are not tightly linked to knowledge of transmission externalities. Only 37 percent of adults understand that worms can spread between household members or neighbors, and the shares who say they would praise someone who deworms and look down on someone who abstains are statistically indistinguishable across communities with above- and below-median externality knowledge and along a continuous knowledge index (Table~\ref{tab:praise-stigma-externality}). Accordingly, throughout the paper we interpret the primary social image motive as signaling health conscientiousness and personal responsibility rather than prosociality.\footnote{This distinction is not critical for our framework: in either case, observability can generate an image return. What matters for the multiplier is how observability and inference vary with distance.}

\paragraph{Adult take-up and program context.}
Despite low monetary costs (a full course costs roughly US\$0.50--2.00 in local pharmacies), few adults deworm regularly: 38 percent report deworming in the previous year, although 69 percent have done so at least once (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-bal}, Panel~B). In high-prevalence areas, adults are targeted through door-to-door visits by Community Health Volunteers (CHVs), but there is no routine program providing free treatment.\footnote{CHVs distribute drugs during periodic MDA rounds, which typically last one to two days.}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Community Deworming Program.}
In partnership with the Kenyan Ministry of Health, we implemented a community deworming campaign that offered free treatment to approximately 200{,}000 adults in Busia, Siaya, and Kakamega counties, where helminths are endemic. The campaign operated for 12 consecutive days, 8 a.m.--5 p.m., at easily identifiable locations staffed by trained CHVs.\footnote{Implementation took place in two waves in 2016: early--mid October (Busia and Siaya) and late October--early November (Kakamega).} A central implementation decision in such programs is how many points of treatment (PoTs) to open and where to locate them: additional PoTs reduce travel costs but require additional fixed operating resources (CHV staffing, training and supervision, and drug logistics). This large-scale implementation created the logistical scope to randomize both travel distance and the observability of participation, forming the core of our experimental design. 

\section{Experimental Design} \label{design}

We implement a factorial design that orthogonally varies, at the cluster level, (i) travel distance to treatment sites and (ii) the observability of participation. A cluster is defined by a point of treatment (PoT) and one targeted community selected from the PoT's catchment. In addition, within each cluster we randomly assign a small subset of phone owners to receive SMS messages that either (i) remind them of the campaign or (ii) remind them and report current local take-up. The cluster level cross-randomization tests our core hypothesis---whether distance changes have different effects when participation is more observable---while the
individual level SMS messages provide a robustness check for reminder and social-information channels. Figure~\ref{fig:design-schematic} summarizes the design and randomization hierarchy.

\subsection{Distance Assignment}\label{sec:distance-treatment}

Implementing the distance treatment required jointly selecting treatment locations and targeted communities under geographic and logistical constraints. We relied on geospatial data on primary schools, churches, and mosques as spatial reference points: areas around these locations provide feasible centralized locations for points of treatment (PoTs), and the dense geographic coverage of primary schools and religious centers in the study area provides a proxy for locating nearby communities, in the absence of geo-coded community locations.

We proceed in three steps: (i) select PoT-centered clusters using a geographically constrained procedure, (ii) randomly assign each selected cluster to a Close or Far distance condition, (iii) randomly select one targeted community per cluster whose centroid lies in the assigned distance band relative to that cluster's PoT. Appendix~\ref{sec:site-selection} provides full details. %algorithmic

We operationalize this feasibility requirement by counting available primary schools/religious centers in the non-overlapping portion of each 2.5~km catchment: a location counts as available if at least 60\% of a 1~km radius circle around it lies within the non-overlapping portion of the catchment. \noindent\textbf{3.\ Targeted community selection.}
To implement the assigned distance category, we selected for each cluster, from its non-overlapping area and according to its distance assignment, a primary school/religious center as an anchor to locate eligible communities to target. \vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Distance as a continuous measure.}
Beyond the Close/Far indicator, we use centroid-to-PoT distance as a continuous measure in the reduced form and structural
analyses, since it directly enters individuals' travel costs (and is common knowledge within the community). Specifically,
we calculate the distance from each community's centroid to its assigned PoT.

Because PoTs are selected using a geographically constrained procedure, the induced assignment mechanism could in principle
depend on local geography (e.g., school density). Following \citet{borusyak2023nonrandom}, we re-run the full selection and
assignment algorithm 100 times and find no evidence that potential centroid-to-PoT distance distributions differ between Close
and Far assignments (Figure~\ref{fig:bh-dens-plot}). We further verify that realized centroid-to-PoT distance is uncorrelated
with baseline covariates (Figure~\ref{fig:ri-baseline}). Taken together, these checks support treating distance as randomly assigned.

\subsection{Incentive Treatments}

We cross-randomize, at the cluster level, incentives that vary the observability of campaign participation:
two publicly visible signals (a bracelet and indelible ink), a private reward with limited public display (a wall calendar), and a control with no item.\footnote{We piloted the incentives to choose acceptable colors and a private reward that is small, useful, and easy to implement. The color green was selected for both bracelets and ink because it avoids association with political parties and was favored during piloting.} Incentives were distributed by CHVs to adults who dewormed at the PoT, making receipt a credible signal of participation. Incentive arms were assigned with equal probability within each county $\times$ distance stratum:

\begin{itemize}  
\item[] \textbf{}
\item \textbf{Bracelet (39 clusters; Close 21, Far 18).}
  Adults received a green silicone bracelet reading ``Treat worms---improve the health of your community'' (Figure~\ref{fig:bracelet}).

  \item \textbf{Ink (36 clusters; Close 21, Far 15).}
  Adults received a green indelible ink mark on the thumb, similar to ink used in elections.\footnote{Our implementing partner was interested in ink due to its near zero unit cost, while bracelets cost approximately US\$0.20 each.}

  \item \textbf{Calendar (35 clusters; Close 19, Far 16).}
  Adults received a simple wall calendar containing no deworming message (Figure~\ref{fig:calendar}).\footnote{Wall calendars are popular decorative items in Kenya and cost approximately US\$0.50 each.}

  \item \textbf{Control (34 clusters; Close 19, Far 15).}
  No item was given upon deworming. \\
\end{itemize}

The Bracelet and Ink arms are designed to increase observability---how easily peers can learn about others' participation---and thus potentially strengthen social image incentives. The Calendar arm provides an active control that delivers a tangible private reward and a personal reminder of participation, but offers limited scope for public display, helping us distinguish the incremental effect of public observability from generic gift or self-image effects. At endline, we elicit willingness to pay to switch between a calendar and a bracelet in Control clusters to quantify their private value and help bound the extent to which Bracelet–Calendar differences reflect private utility rather than observability.

\subsubsection{Reminder and Social Information Treatment}
The majority of adults in our study area own a phone (75\%, see Table
\ref{tab:baseline-bal}). We randomly assign SMS messages to a small share of phone owners (fewer than 10\%) to test whether reminders or social learning mechanisms help explain our treatment effects. Using the household census, we randomly sampled phone owners for SMS assignment: in each Control cluster, we sampled 15 phone owners for a Reminder Only SMS and 15 phone owners for a Social Info SMS; in each Incentive cluster, we sampled 25 phone owners for the Social Info SMS. Importantly, sampling was done without replacement at the household level: once an individual was selected for either SMS treatment arm, the entire household was removed from the SMS sampling frame. As a result, no household could have more than one adult assigned to SMS treatment, and no household with an SMS-treated adult could contribute a different adult to the SMS control group. Enumerators recruited selected individuals in-person prior to the campaign. In total, 3{,}022 adults enrolled in the SMS service: 387 adults in Reminder Only and 2{,}635 in Social Info.\footnote{Among adults reached for recruitment, 18 declined enrollment. Enrollment fell short of per cluster targets primarily because some sampled adults could not be reached during recruitment visits. Enrollment is balanced across incentive arms, with about 20 Social Info recipients per incentive cluster. In Control clusters, Reminder Only and Social Info each average about 11--12 recipients.}

The Reminder Only message reminded individuals about the availability of free deworming at the local PoT, while the Social Info message additionally reported the current local take-up rate.\footnote{Reminder Only: ``Free
deworming now at [Central Location]. Reply 111 to stop texts. All texts are free.'' Social Info: ``Free deworming now at [Central Location]. [No/few/almost half/half/more than half/almost all/all] of your village came, that is X in 10
adults. Reply 111 to stop texts. All texts are free.''} Enrolled adults received a first SMS the evening before the campaign and follow-up messages on campaign days 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10. The service was free and recipients could unsubscribe at any time at no cost. \subsection{Information Outreach and Incentive Disclosure}\label{sec:info-campaign} 

One week before the deworming campaign, Community Health Volunteers (CHVs) visited each of the 144 selected study communities to announce the program. These visits served two purposes: (i) ensure high awareness of campaign dates and locations, and (ii) publicly disclose the incentive that would be provided upon deworming in that area.

CHVs are trusted local health workers who live in the communities they serve and support a range of public health activities, including Kenya’s school-based deworming program. During community visits, CHVs followed a standardized script that (i) announced the campaign dates and PoT location, (ii) described the assigned incentive, and (iii) explained that regular deworming---even in the absence of symptoms---benefits both children and adults and can reduce worm transmission within the community (Figure~\ref{fig:sensitization} reproduces the script). In addition, CHVs distributed flyers and posted posters displaying the campaign dates, location, and the relevant incentive (Figures~\ref{fig:flyers}). These materials 
served as visual reminders and helped coordinate expectations about what the bracelet, ink mark, or calendar signified.

\subsection{Empirical Strategy}

We estimate the effects of distance and incentive treatments on (i) the observability of deworming and (ii) individual take‑up.  For individual $i$ in community $c$ we estimate:

\begin{align*}
 Y_{ic}  = \alpha_s + \sum \beta_z \textrm{treat}_{z,ic} + 
    \sum \gamma_z \textrm{treat}_{z,ic} \times \textrm{Far}_{ic} +
    X_{ic} + \varepsilon_{ic} \label{eq:rf} 
\end{align*}

where $Y_{ic}$ is either (a) the share of peers whose deworming status respondent $i$ reports knowing or (b) a binary indicator equal to 1 if $i$ dewormed.  $\textrm{treat}_{z,ic}$ denotes indicators for the Control, Calendar, Ink, and Bracelet arms, where $z$ indexes each arm. $\textrm{Far}_{ic}$ equals 1 for communities assigned to the Far distance condition (1.25–2.5 km) and 0 otherwise. The interaction terms capture differential incentive effects at greater distance. $X_{ic}$ includes baseline covariates selected via LASSO (sex and age) and the community’s expected distance to the PoT computed using the method of \citet{borusyak2023nonrandom}.  $\alpha_s$ denotes strata fixed effects, and $\varepsilon_{ic}$ is an idiosyncratic error.  Standard errors are cluster‑bootstrap estimates at the community level.

We first examine how travel distance and signaling incentives jointly affect whether an individual’s deworming status is known within the community.

At endline, respondents were shown a random subset of ten adults from the same community and asked, conditional on recognition, “Do you think this person came for deworming?”.  We code “Yes” and “No” as 1 (status known) and “Don’t know” as 0 (status unknown).  Observability is the respondent‑level share of recognized peers whose status is known; it captures the ease with which deworming can be observed or inferred through direct sight, communication, or close familiarity.

%This measure conditions on the locally observable network (the peers each respondent recognizes). Recognition rates do not differ across incentive arms or by distance, and results are robust to weighting observations by the number recognized ($r_i$) or estimating a binomial GLM with $k_i$ successes out of $r_i$ trials (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:recog-balance}).

Deworming decisions are highly observable in a campaign setting: Control group respondents know the status of 69.6 percent of recognized peers (Table \ref{tab:beliefs-visibility}).  Knowledge is higher in Close communities (75.1 percent) than in Far communities (63.1 percent, $p = 0.056$). Endline qualitative data point to two mechanisms (Figure \ref{fig:sob-reasons}): (i) greater proximity to PoTs increases direct encounters and conversation; (ii) lower take‑up in Far communities reduces opportunities for observation. Absent physically seeing a peer traveling to the PoT or present there, it is hard to discern whether the peer failed to deworm or whether the deworming simply went unobserved.

Signaling incentives raise observability, especially in Far communities.  The Bracelet and Ink treatments increase knowledge of peers’ status by 12.6 percentage points ($p = 0.001$) and 8.1 points ($p = 0.046$), respectively.  The treatment effects are sharply heterogeneous: for the Bracelet, observability rises by 22 percentage points in Far communities ($p<0.001$) but by only 4.8 points in Close communities ($p=0.357$); for the Ink, the corresponding increases are 14.8 points in Far communities ($p=0.007$) and 2.6 points in Close communities ($p=0.644$). Because obtaining the signal is costlier in Far communities, the signal likely conveys stronger information about an individual’s motivation and is more actively displayed, resulting in larger gains in observability. 

As expected, the Calendar has a limited effect on observability, increasing it by 3.5 percentage points ($p = 0.382$). The difference between the Calendar and Bracelet arms is significant ($p < 0.05$) in all comparisons except for Close communities ($p = 0.826$).

Combining the Bracelet and Ink arms (Any Signal) and comparing them with the Calendar and Control arms (No Signal), we find that signals offset the negative distance gradient in observability by 15 percentage points ($p = 0.002$). Table \ref{tab:rf-fob-tes-cts-dist} shows that the results are robust when distance enters the specification as a continuous variable. 


\paragraph{Correct observability and accuracy.}
While Table~\ref{tab:beliefs-visibility} measures observability as the share of recognized peers for whom respondents provide a definite (directional) belief (Yes/No) rather than answering ``Don't know'', this does not imply that such beliefs are correct. Using administrative take-up records for the referenced peers, we therefore construct a correct observability measure---the share of recognized peers that respondents correctly classify---and decompose it into (i) the share of dyads for which respondents provide a definite (directional) belief and (ii) accuracy conditional on providing a definite (directional) belief (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:decomp_1141base}).\footnote{A definite (directional) belief is a Yes/No response and does not imply full certainty.}

The decomposition shows that the Control Far--Close gap is driven largely by a lower propensity to provide definite (directional) beliefs in Far communities, reflected in more frequent ``Don't know'' responses, rather than lower conditional accuracy (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:decomp_1141base}, Panels~A--B). Signals (bracelet and ink) substantially increase the share of dyads for which respondents provide a definite (directional) belief in Far communities (Panel~A), while conditional accuracy declines (Panel~B). This pattern is consistent with additional low-information dyads moving from ``Don't know'' to Yes/No, along with imperfect or noisy signals (e.g., fading ink, intermittent display, or imperfect recall of who received an item). Net effects on correct observability are nevertheless positive in Far because the increase in definite (directional) beliefs leads respondents to correctly classify more peers overall, even if the marginal additional classifications are less accurate than in Control (Panel~C).%should i add concrete numbers? nahhhh this is robusteness

\paragraph{Checks for experimenter demand effects and social desirability bias.} Because our observability outcome is constructed from respondents’ stated beliefs about specific named peers, a natural concern is experimenter demand effects or social desirability bias influencing outcome: respondents may feel pressure to provide a definite answer rather than ``Don’t know'' or to overstate peers’ participation. To probe these concerns, we randomized endline respondents into an alternative elicitation format in which respondents are shown pairs of names, instead of individual names of peers, and first report how many peers they recognize without identifying which. Respondents are then asked whether the person(s) they knew came for deworming (Yes/No/Don’t know) without revealing who they know. The pair format increases reported recognition per name by 11 percentage points, consistent with reduced privacy pressure. However, we find no evidence that the direct named individual format reduces ``Don’t know'' responses or inflates false positives among true non-participants. Instead, the individual format mainly reduces false negatives among true participants. Overall, these checks suggest that our main observability results are unlikely to be driven by experimenter demand or social desirability bias (Appendix~\ref{app:demand_checks}).%

    What these results suggest (and why accuracy changes)

The big takeaway is:

Signals (bracelet/ink) increase reported knowledge (Table 1), but when you look only at cases where people give a Yes/No belief, their Yes/No beliefs are not more accurate — if anything, they are often less accurate (especially in Far).

Here are the main data-backed reasons:

1) The signal arms push people from “Don’t know” into “Yes/No”

This shows up strongly in the dyad data (recognized + truth observed):
	•	In Far control, the “Don’t know” rate is ~35%.
	•	In Far bracelet, “Don’t know” drops to ~14–15%.
	•	In Far ink, it drops to ~18–19%.

So, a large part of “observability increasing” is: people stop saying DK and start committing to Yes/No.

That can increase the fraction correct overall (because some guesses are right), even if the accuracy of Yes/No answers falls.

2) Conditional accuracy falls because the marginal “new” Yes/No answers are lower-quality

A very consistent pattern in your data is:
	•	Control conditional accuracy is about 0.66.
	•	In the signal arms, conditional accuracy is lower, particularly in Far:
	•	Bracelet Far is about 10pp lower than control Far (≈ -0.10).
	•	Calendar Far is also negative (though calendar isn’t a wearable signal — more on that below).
	•	Ink is especially negative in Close (≈ -0.10).

Interpretation: the treatment makes people more willing to answer, but the added answers come from cases where they were uncertain, so those answers are less accurate.

This is exactly the pattern you’d expect from a mix of:
	•	reduced privacy concerns (“it’s okay to answer”)
	•	experimenter demand / social desirability to avoid DK
	•	and/or inference from imperfect cues

3) Errors look like over‑prediction of “Yes” (false positives)

Among dyads where respondents give Yes/No beliefs, respondents tend to say “Yes” more often than the truth rate, particularly in Far.

Example pattern (Far, definite answers):
	•	In control Far, yes responses exceed true take‑up (optimism).
	•	In ink Far, yes responses exceed true take‑up by even more (lots of false positives).

That’s consistent with:
	•	respondents inferring that the campaign was broadly successful (“people probably came”),
	•	or feeling it is more socially acceptable to answer “yes” than “no/don’t know”.

4) Far vs Close: signals mainly change response behavior where baseline knowledge is low

In Far:
	•	baseline “DK” is high,
	•	baseline take-up is lower in control,
	•	and the signals may be rarer/more salient.

So Far is where tokens most strongly move people into “Yes/No,” which drives the big Table 1 observability effects — but also where “Yes/No” answers can be most error-prone.

5) Calendar being negative in Far is informative

Calendar is not a wearable/visible badge the way bracelet/ink is. The fact that calendar Far conditional accuracy is also negative suggests that part of what’s happening is not only “seeing a signal on others”, but also:
	•	a general shift in how respondents answer the knowledge module under “incentive framing,”
	•	possibly “I should give an answer” / reduced DK / social desirability.

That’s useful for referees: it points to a potential demand/response effect component in “observability” self-reports.

6) Selection caveat (important to state in the paper)

Because the conditional-accuracy DV is only defined for respondents with \ge 1 eligible Yes/No belief, and because treatments increase the probability of giving any Yes/No, the set of respondents contributing to the DV changes by arm (selection).

Practically: in control Far, fewer respondents ever give a definite belief; in bracelet Far, many more do. If the additional respondents are less informed, that mechanically lowers average conditional accuracy.

This is why, in the paper, it’s strongest to show a decomposition:
	1.	Claim rate (your Table 1 observability)
	2.	Accuracy conditional on claim (these tables)
	3.	Unconditional correctness (DK counted as wrong), which does not have the same selection issue and often rises in Far under bracelet/ink because DK collapses.

\subsubsection{Effects of Distance and Signals on Deworming Take-up}

Table \ref{tab:overall_effects} reports average treatment effects on the share of adults who dewormed. Even in a large, community‑wide campaign, take‑up is far from universal: only 33.8 percent of adults in the Control arm attended treatment. Consistent with the idea that travel distance imposes a non‑trivial cost, 41 percent of adults in Close communities dewormed, whereas 24.8 percent did so in Far communities—a decline of 16.2 percentage points ($p<0.001$). Respondents’ beliefs move in tandem: in the Control arm they expect 12.1 percentage points fewer adults to deworm in Far than in Close communities (Table \ref{tab:pred-te}).

Providing a bracelet increases take‑up by 7.5 percentage points relative to Control ($p = 0.008$). The Bracelet–Calendar gap of 4.8 percentage points ($p = 0.048$) provides a naïve estimate of the social image component. Calendar effects are identical in Close and Far communities (–0.0 pp, $p = 0.959$), implying that the 16 pp distance gradient persists when no public signal is offered. Bracelet raises take‑up by 6 pp in Close communities ($p = 0.065$), and the Ink interaction with distance becomes larger (7.6 pp, $p = 0.067$).  The difference‑in‑difference test of whether signals are differentially effective at greater distances is significant at the 5 percent level ($p = 0.049$).


To visualize these interactions in the raw data, Figure~\ref{fig:np-rf} plots community level take-up against centroid-to-site distance, fitting arm specific penalized splines. Take-up declines with distance in all arms, but the decline is markedly steeper in the lower-visibility arms (Control/Calendar) than when deworming is publicly signaled (Bracelet/Ink), so the fitted curves diverge with distance. This conclusion is unchanged if we compare the bracelet to the calendar, and it holds separately in Close and Far communities.

\paragraph{Externality Motives.} A related channel is that increased observability shifts beliefs about community coverage and opportunities to free ride on infection spillovers. In a standard free-riding logic, an individual’s private return to deworming is decreasing in the perceived share of others who deworm: higher expected coverage implies lower infection risk and therefore weaker private incentives to treat. Increased observability could therefore raise or lower take-up depending on whether it leads adults to believe that coverage is lower or higher. Empirically, however, the signal arms raise take-up while shifting beliefs in the opposite direction one would expect under free riding: relative to Control, respondents in the signal arms report higher expected community take-up (Table~\ref{tab:pred-te}). Moreover, knowledge that worm infections can spread to others changes little and does not increase differentially in Far communities, where the signals raise observability most (Table~\ref{tab:endline-deworm-knowledge}). Taken together, these patterns are difficult to reconcile with externality motives as the primary driver of the distance $\times$ signal interaction.


\subsubsection{Private Incentive Explanations}\label{subsec:consumption}

A remaining set of alternatives is that the distance $\times$ signal interaction reflects private incentives rather than social image. We assess two such channels using the calendar arm and endline willingness-to-pay (WTP) and gift choice evidence. 

\paragraph{Density at the Participation Threshold.} In a standard latent-index model with an additive incentive, treatment effects scale with the density of individuals near the participation cutoff. A pure ``mass near the margin'' mechanism therefore predicts that any additive incentive should have larger effects in Far communities, not only incentives that are publicly visible. The calendar arm provides a direct placebo: its effect on take-up is similar in Close and Far communities, while the distance interaction arises only for the signals. This pattern is inconsistent with a higher density near the margin explanation as the primary driver of the distance $\times$ signal interaction.

\paragraph{Distance Varying Private Value.} We next examine whether the stronger Far effects could reflect higher private valuation of the bracelet (relative to the calendar) among adults at farther distances. We begin with an endline WTP exercise in Control communities. Seventy-three percent of respondents prefer the calendar, and the implied mean value gap is US\$0.47 in favor of the calendar (Table~\ref{tab:wtp-summ-table}). Thus, on average, the bracelet does not dominate the calendar in private value. 

The relevant concern, however, is valuation among adults close to participating at different distances. We therefore look at endline gift choices among adults who did not deworm as our most relevant proxy for marginal participants. In Control communities---where neither item is locally prevalent---bracelet choice shares among non-dewormers are stable across distance: 24.9\% choose the bracelet in Close and 22.2\% in Far communities (Table~\ref{tab:pref-cal-gift}). This provides no evidence that marginal adults at longer distances have a higher relative private valuation for the bracelet. 

Because the Control arm WTP exercise samples only a few respondents per community, it may miss utility components that depend on local item prevalence. We therefore examine endline gift choices among non-dewormers in Bracelet and Calendar communities (who did not already receive an item), where local prevalence of each item is salient. Table~\ref{tab:pref-cal-gift} shows clear prevalence effects: when the calendar is locally common (Calendar arm), non-dewormers are more likely to choose the bracelet than in Control (+15.9 pp, \(p<0.01\)); when the bracelet is locally common (Bracelet arm), non-dewormers are less likely to choose the bracelet than in Control (-6.6 pp,\(p<0.05\)), driven by Close communities (−13.6 pp) with essentially no change in Far (−0.2 pp). If longer distances generated a scarcity premium in the bracelet's private value---i.e., made it privately more attractive than baseline---then non-dewormers in Bracelet--Far should be more likely to choose the bracelet than comparable adults in Control--Far. Instead, bracelet choice in Bracelet--Far is statistically indistinguishable from Control--Far (Table~\ref{tab:pref-cal-gift}). Overall, the WTP and gift choice evidence provides little support for a private-value-only account of the stronger Far effects of the signals.

Finally, Appendix~\ref{sec:mrs} formalizes a related private cost channel: if the distance $\times$ signal interaction were driven by distance varying substitution between travel costs and private incentives, then the relative private valuation of the bracelet versus the calendar would become more favorable at farther distances, given the calendar arm's take-up effect is essentially flat across distance. Empirically, we find no such pattern: relative preferences for the bracelet versus the calendar are invariant to distance (Table~\ref{tab:pref-cal-gift}). Thus, heterogeneous substitution alone cannot account for the stronger Far effects of the signals.


\subsection{Summary}

The reduced form evidence delivers two central findings. First, the distance assignment generates exogenous variation in both take-up and observability: moving a community from Close to Far lowers deworming by about 16 percentage points and makes participation less observable. Second, the signaling arms increase observability and raise participation relative to the no-signaling arms, and the Far--Close drop in take-up is meaningfully smaller with public signals than without. We also find little support for reminder/learning channels, externality-based motives, or distance-varying private valuation as primary explanations for both the average increase in take‑up and the disproportionate effect of signals at greater distances.

Even if social image is the driver, the reduced form alone does not separate the two components of image returns: distance can change both how likely participation is observed and what being observed implies about motivation. These results motivate a simple framework adapted from \citet{Benabou_laws_2011} in which deworming yields private benefits and a social image return that decomposes into (i) the probability that participation is observed and (ii) the social image value of being observed. Distance and the signals can affect both components. In the structural model that follows, we use the observability data to quantify the respective roles of observability and belief updating.

\section{Theoretical Framework} \label{model}

In this section we take the standard social image model of Bénabou and Tirole (2006, 2011) and specialize it to our deworming setting. We do not introduce new theoretical primitives; instead, we use their framework as an organizing device to map the experimental objects in Sections 3–4 into three components:%weird to say experimental objects, needed?
(i) private benefits net of travel costs, (ii) the observability of deworming as a function of distance and the signaling arm, and (iii) the belief-based return to being perceived as a more motivated type. The model serves two purposes. First, it clarifies how distance and observability jointly shape equilibrium deworming decisions, guiding our interpretation of the reduced form evidence. Second, it provides a disciplined way to construct and estimate the social multiplier—the ratio of the distance effect on take-up when actions are observable to the distance effect when they are not—and to embed that multiplier in the siting problem in Section 7. %Section 7 reference needed? 

We adapt the signaling framework of \citet{Benabou_laws_2011} for two purposes. First, it delivers predictions for how cost and observability jointly interact to determine deworming take-up, guiding our interpretation of the reduced form evidence in Section~\ref{reduced-form-results}. Second, it provides the structure for Section~\ref{structural}, where we quantify the \emph{social multiplier}—the proportional responsiveness of take-up to distance when social image concerns are present, relative to a private cost benchmark.


\subsection{Deworming with Social Image Concerns}

\textbf{Setup.} We follow Bénabou and Tirole (2006, 2011) and model the deworming decision as a simple binary choice with social image concerns.
Individual $i$ chooses whether to deworm, $y_i \in \{0,1\}$, trading off private benefits net of travel costs against a reputational payoff from being observed deworming rather than abstaining. Utility is given by 
\begin{equation}
U_i = (b - d + v_i + u_i)y_i  +  \mu(d) E_{-i}[V | y_i] \nonumber
\end{equation} 
where $b - d$ is the net private payoff, combining the health (and material incentive) benefit \(b\) with a distance-induced travel cost $d\ge0$. Intrinsic motivation $v_i$—health conscientiousness or civic‑mindedness—is privately known and unobservable to others. The idiosyncratic shock \(u_i\) captures taste variations or one‑off costs and is independent of \(v_i\).  Since both variables are latent, we define the composite type \(w_i \equiv v_i + u_i\). \(\mu(d)\) is the weight placed on social image and may itself depend on distance, while \(E_{-i}[V \mid y_i]\) is the expectation that observers form about $v_i$ after seeing action $y_i$. Following \citet{Benabou_laws_2011}, \(\mu(d)\) bundles two elements: the physical observability of the act and the individual’s valuation of social image (their parameter $\lambda$). In our context, observability is empirically lower in Far than in Close communities, so we allow $\mu(d)$ to vary with distance.

%Observability is empirically much lower in Far than in Close communities, so we allow \(\mu(d)\) to decline with distance.  Conversely, if performing a costlier act makes the signal more valuable and actively displayed, \(\mu(d)\) could rise with $d$; the model accommodates either pattern. \\


\textbf{Equilibrium.} Following \citet{Benabou_incentives_2006, Benabou_laws_2011}, a unique equilibrium exists under observability where each individual’s choice \(y_i\) depends on the equilibrium actions of all other individuals. 
Equilibrium behavior is characterized by a cut-off type \(w^{\ast}(d)\) such that an individual with \(w_i > w^{\ast}(d)\) deworms and one with \(w_i \le w^{\ast}(d)\) abstains.  The cut-off satisfies the fixed‑point equation:\footnote{The cut-off \(w^{\ast}(d)\) depends on the primitives \((b,d,\mu(d))\). Our comparative static results focus on \(d\), treating the other parameters as fixed.}
\begin{align*}\label{eq:cutoff}
w^\ast(d) - d + b + \mu(d) \Delta(w^\ast(d)) = 0
\end{align*}
where $\Delta(w^\ast(d)) = E[V | w > w^\ast(d)] - E[V | w \leq w^\ast(d)]$ is the difference in expected intrinsic motivation between those who deworm and those who abstain. Hence the social image return is the product of observability and type inference, $\mu(d) \Delta(w^\ast(d))$. 
Given the cut-off, the equilibrium participation rate at distance \(d\) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:takeup-prop}
\bar{y}(d) = 1 - F_w\left(w^{\ast}(d) \right)
\end{equation}
where \(F_w\) is the cumulative distribution function of \(w\).

\textbf{Social Image Returns and Cut-off Type.} Figure~\ref{fig:delta1} illustrates how social image returns, $\mu(d)\,\Delta\!\bigl(w^{\ast}(d)\bigr)$, change as the cut-off type \(w^\ast(d)\)—and thus the participation rate—varies while $\mu(d)$ is held fixed. When $w^{\ast}(d)$ is near zero (``modal'' acts) an individual’s decision conveys little information about their type, so $\Delta$ is small. For ``respectable'' acts ($w^{\ast}\!\ll 0$) almost everyone deworms except the very lowest types, producing strong type inferences and increasing $\Delta$.  For ``admirable'' acts ($w^{\ast}\!\gg 0$) only the most motivated deworm, again raising $\Delta$. Because deworming is a minority behavior in our data, the relevant region is likely the admirable range.

Allowing for idiosyncratic shocks $u_i$ blurs type inferences.  When the variance $\sigma_{u}$ is small (lighter curves at the top of Figure \ref{fig:delta1}), the shock contributes little in expectation and actions closely reveal $v_i$. As $\sigma_{u}$ rises, observers cannot tell whether an individual deworms because of intrinsic motivation or because of a favorable shock. Social image returns fall at every cut-off $w^\ast$, and the U-shaped returns curve progressively flattens.\footnote{The curve flattens rather than merely shifts downward. Holding $v$ fixed, an increase in $\sigma_u$ has its greatest impact in the tails of the $w^\ast$ distribution, where a larger share of individuals act because of noise rather than high motivation.} 


Figure \ref{fig:delta2} allows observability to fall with distance, $\mu'(d)\!<0$.  If the decline in $\mu(d)$ dominates the rise in $\Delta$, the U‑shape can invert, producing a downwards sloping pattern: at very large $d$, only the most motivated individuals deworm, yet their virtue is largely hidden because few witness the act, and the social image return $\mu(d)\,\Delta\!\bigl(w^{\ast}(d)\bigr)$ becomes small. \\


\textbf{The Social Multiplier.} A social multiplier different from one arises because the cut-off type--and hence social image returns--shifts when distance changes. To see this, differentiate the equilibrium participation rate in \eqref{eq:takeup-prop} with respect to $d$:

\begin{align*}\label{eq:sm}
    \frac{\partial \bar{y}}{\partial d} &= -f_w(w^\ast(d)) \underbrace{\frac{1 - \mu'(d) \Delta(w^\ast(d))}{1 + \mu(d) \Delta'(w^\ast(d))}}_{\partial w^\ast/\partial d}.  
\end{align*}

Two endogenous channels accompany the direct cost term $-f_w(w^{\ast})$:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Type inference effect $\Delta’(\cdot)$: A higher cost filters out less motivated individuals; if this makes deworming appear more virtuous so that $\Delta’(\cdot)>0$, the social image payoff rises with distance and partly mitigates the direct cost effect.  By contrast, if a marginal increase in distance pushes the activity toward the modal range so that $\Delta’(\cdot)<0$, the social image payoff falls and amplifies the direct cost effect.

\item Observability effect $\mu'(\cdot)$: Greater distance may reduce the observability of actions, $\mu(d)$, and thus amplify the cost effect.
 
\end{enumerate}

Whether distance mitigates or amplifies its own direct impact depends on $\partial w^{\ast}/\partial d$.  If the cut-off shifts more than one‑for‑one (\,$\partial w^{\ast}/\partial d>1$\,), amplification occurs; if it shifts less than one‑for‑one (\,$\partial w^{\ast}/\partial d<1$\,), the cost effect is mitigated. \\


\textbf{Existence of a Social Multiplier.} A social multiplier is present whenever the cut-off does not adjust one‑for‑one with distance, that is \(\partial w^{\ast}/\partial d \neq 1\).  Two special cases instead yield \(\partial w^{\ast}/\partial d = 1\), so the multiplier equals one. 

The first is a knife-edge case: if the marginal decrease in observability exactly offsets the marginal increase in type inference, $-\mu’(d)\,\Delta\bigl(w^{\ast}(d)\bigr)=\mu(d)\,\Delta’\bigl(w^{\ast}(d)\bigr)$, the two forces cancel, so the cut-off shifts one‑for‑one with distance.

The second case is when social image returns are locally flat. Formally, this requires:  
$\mu'(d)=0$ and $\Delta'\!\bigl(w^{\ast}(d)\bigr)=0$. Because $\mu'(d)=0$ whenever observability does not vary with distance, the critical restriction is $\Delta'=0$.  Recall $\Delta(w^{\ast}) = E[V \mid w>w^{\ast}] - E[V \mid w\le w^{\ast}],$ the difference between the two “centers of mass’’ defined by the cut-off $w^{\ast}$.  In a non‑uniform density $f_w$, moving the cut-off changes those centers unequally, so $\Delta$ typically changes. $\Delta$ can be locally flat only if $f_w$ is (approximately) uniform in a neighborhood of the cut-off, causing both centers to shift in parallel and leave their gap unchanged.  A fully uniform density gives $\Delta(w^{\ast})\equiv c$ for all $w^{\ast}$; a symmetric density does so only at $w^{\ast}=0$ where marginal gains in “honor’’ equal marginal losses in “stigma’’.

Figure \ref{fig:delta1} illustrates the uniform density limit: as the variance of the idiosyncratic shock $\sigma_u$ becomes large, the composite type $w=v+u$ becomes dominated by the noise term, and its probability density flattens toward a uniform distribution. Consequently $\Delta(w^{\ast})$ approaches a constant, $\Delta’(w^{\ast})\to 0$, and---since $\mu’(d)=0$---the social multiplier converges to one. Introducing the shock \(u_{i}\) nests both the benchmark of \citet{Benabou_laws_2011} and the constant return case, leaving the model agnostic about the multiplier’s magnitude.  In Section \ref{structural} we estimate \(\sigma_{u}\) directly. If the data were to imply a very large value, \(\Delta'\) would be small and the implied multiplier close to one (i.e., negligible).

\subsection{Predictions}

To connect the model to the reduced form evidence in Section~\ref{reduced-form-results}, we state three theoretical predictions. 

\begin{prediction}\label{pred:cost} {\rm \textbf{Direct Cost Effect:}} An increase in travel distance $d$ raises the private cost of deworming, thereby reducing aggregate take-up:\footnote{Following \cite{Benabou_laws_2011}, we assume that the multiplier is positive -- this holds true provided social image returns are not too large $1 - \mu' \Delta(w^\ast(d)) > 0$. Figure \ref{fig:dydc-robust-check} verifies this condition for all distances we consider, using estimates from our structural model.}
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial \bar{y}}{\partial d} &= - f_w(w^\ast(d)) \underbrace{\frac{1 - \mu' \Delta(w^\ast(d))}{1 + \mu \Delta'(w^\ast(d))}}_{\partial w^\ast / \partial d} < 0 \label{eq:marginal-effect-of-cost}
\end{align*}

\end{prediction}

Consistent with this prediction, take‑up is 16 percentage points lower in Far than in Close communities.


\begin{prediction}\label{pred:visibility} {\rm \textbf{Direct Observability Effect:}} Holding distance fixed, higher observability $\mu$ raises social image returns and increases take‑up:
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial \bar{y}}{\partial \mu} &= f_w(w^\ast(d)) \underbrace{\frac{\Delta(w^\ast(d))}{1 + \mu\Delta’(w^\ast(d))}}_{\partial w ^\ast/\partial \mu} > 0
\end{align*}
\end{prediction}

Consistent with this, the high-visibility Bracelet arm yields markedly higher participation than the low‑visibility Calendar or Control arms.


\begin{prediction}\label{pred:amp-mit-mu}
{\rm \textbf{Indirect Effect of Distance - Amplification versus  Mitigation}}: If the semi‑elasticity of the type‑inference term exceeds that of observability,
\[
\frac{\partial\Delta/\partial d}{\Delta}
>
-\frac{\partial\mu/\partial d}{\mu},
\]
then a marginal increase in distance is mitigated:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mit}
\frac{\partial\bar y}{\partial d}
\;=\;
-\,f_w\!\bigl(w^{\ast}(d)\bigr)\,
       \frac{1-\mu'(d)\,\Delta(w^{\ast}(d))}
            {1+\mu(d)\,\Delta'(w^{\ast}(d))}  
\;>\;
-\,f_w\!\bigl(w^{\ast}(d)\bigr). \nonumber 
\end{equation}

Conversely, if the semi‑elasticity of observability dominates,
\[
-\frac{\partial\mu/\partial d}{\mu}
>
\frac{\partial\Delta/\partial d}{\Delta},
\]
the distance effect is amplified:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:amp}
\frac{\partial\bar y}{\partial d}
\;=\;
-\,f_w\!\bigl(w^{\ast}(d)\bigr)\,
       \frac{1-\mu'(d)\,\Delta(w^{\ast}(d))}
            {1+\mu(d)\,\Delta'(w^{\ast}(d))}  
\;<\;
-\,f_w\!\bigl(w^{\ast}(d)\bigr). \nonumber 
\end{equation}
\end{prediction}

Our experimental results show that the negative effect of distance on deworming take‑up is substantially smaller when observability is high—Bracelet or Ink—than when it is low, as in the Calendar or Control arms.

\section{Structural Estimates} \label{structural}

This section presents analyses that were not prespecified in the preregistered plan (AEARCTR‑0001643; see Section~\ref{sec:prereg} and Appendix Table~\ref{tab:pap_mapping}). These are theory driven extensions intended to interpret—rather than replace—the preregistered reduced form estimates.

We take the model from Section \ref{model} to the data to obtain explicit estimates of the social multiplier. The analysis proceeds in four steps. First, we map the theory into three interlocking submodels: (i) a deworming submodel fit to 9,805 monitored take‑up observations; (ii) an observability submodel estimated on belief observations from 999 respondents; and (iii) a willingness to pay (WTP) submodel based on 998 valuation choices. Because the take‑up data alone cannot identify social image and preference parameters, the deworming submodel takes those parameters from the observability and WTP submodels. We combine the three components in a joint likelihood so that the resulting posterior must simultaneously rationalize all observed outcomes. Second, we show that the model implied average treatment effects replicate the reduced form evidence. Third, we decompose each treatment effect into its social image and private valuation components. Finally, we report the social multiplier estimates for each treatment arm. \\

\textbf{Deworming Take-up Model.} We parameterize utility using indicator variables for the incentive arms, \(\text{treat}_{z,i}\), and each individual's community distance to the treatment site, \(d_i\):
\begin{equation}
U_i = (\beta_z \textrm{treat}_{z,i} - \delta d_i + w_i)y_i  +  \mu(\textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i) E_{-i}[V | y_i].
\label{eq:struct-utility}
\end{equation} 
Here \(\beta_z\) captures the private value of incentive \(z\in\{\text{Control},\text{Ink},\text{Calendar},\text{Bracelet}\}\);  \(\delta>0\) is the marginal disutility of distance; and \(\mu(\cdot)\) is the weight on social image.  We write \(w_i=v_i+u_i\) and assume \(w_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_u^{2}+1)\), normalizing \(\operatorname{Var}(v_i)=1\).%
\footnote{Although error variances are typically unidentified in latent-index models, \(\sigma_u\) enters the conditional mean through \(\Delta(\cdot)\) permitting identification here.}

Following \citet{Benabou_laws_2011}, we factor observability as
\begin{align*}
\mu(\textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i) = \lambda \cdot p_{\textrm{Observed}}(\textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i),    
\end{align*}
where \(p_{\text{Observed}}(\cdot)\) is the probability that an individual’s deworming status is known in the community. We estimate \(p_{\text{Observed}}\) by a logit model using the endline beliefs data; \(\lambda\) is a scalar converting observability into utility. 

The equilibrium cut-off satisfies the fixed-point equation
\begin{align}
    w^\ast(\textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i) - \delta d_i + \beta_z \textrm{treat}_{z,i} + \mu(\textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i) \Delta \left( w^\ast(\textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i\right)) = 0.
    \label{eq:w-fp-stat}
\end{align}
Since \(w^{\ast}\bigl(\text{treat}_{z,i},d_i\bigr)\) is the type exactly indifferent between deworming and abstaining, the probability that individual \(i\) deworms under treatment \(z\) at distance \(d_i\) is 
\begin{align*}
    P(y_i = 1 | \textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i) &= 1 - \Phi\left(\frac{w^\ast(\textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i)}{\sqrt{ \sigma_u^2 + 1}}\right), \\
\end{align*}
where \(\Phi(\cdot)\) denotes the standard normal c.d.f. Accordingly, the individual likelihood contribution is  
 \begin{align*}   
    \mathcal{L}(\beta_z, \delta, \lambda, \sigma_u | 
    y_i,
    \textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i) &= \left[1 - \Phi\left(\frac{w^\ast(\textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i)}{\sqrt{ \sigma_u^2 + 1}}\right)\right]^{y_i} \times \left[
    \Phi\left(\frac{w^\ast(\textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i)}{\sqrt{ \sigma_u^2 + 1}}\right)
    \right]^{1 - y_i}
    \label{eq:lh-takeup}
\end{align*}
with \(w^{\ast}=w^{\ast}\bigl(\text{treat}_{z,i},d_i\bigr)\) implicitly defined by equation \eqref{eq:w-fp-stat}. Thus, the structural parameters \((\beta,\delta,\lambda,\sigma_u)\) enter the likelihood only through their impact on the cut-off \(w^{\ast}\).\\

\textbf{Observability Model.} We model the probability that an individual’s deworming status is known within the community as a logit function of incentive arm and travel distance:

\begin{align*}
    {p_{\textrm{Observed}}(\textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i)} &= \textrm{logit}^{-1}\left(\beta^O_z \textrm{treat}_{z,i} + \gamma_z^O \textrm{treat}_{z,i} \times d_i\right)  
\end{align*}
At endline each respondent \(i\) identifies \(r_i\) recognizable adults and reports knowing the deworming status of \(k_i\le r_i\) of them. Conditional on treatments, \((\text{treat}_{z,i},d_i)\), we treat \(k_i\) as binomial:
\begin{align*}    
    \mathcal{L}(p_{\textrm{Observed}} | k_i, r_i) &= \binom{r_i}{k_i} \times p_{\textrm{Observed}}(\textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i)^{k_i} \times ( 1 - p_{\textrm{Observed}}(\textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i))^{r_i-k_i}. 
    \label{eq:binom-likelihood} 
\end{align*}


The logit specification guarantees \(p_{\text{Observed}}\in[0,1]\), a property that disciplines counterfactual simulations in the structural model.  The binomial likelihood naturally up‑weights respondents who recognize more community members (\(r_i\) large) and hence provide more information about observability. \\

\textbf{Preference (WTP) Model.} We use the bracelet–calendar preference experiment to discipline the private value parameters of the two incentives. The elicitation had two stages. First, each respondent chose a gift—bracelet or calendar. Second, the enumerator offered a random cash amount \(m_i\in[0,1]\) (USD) to switch to the alternative gift.

We adopt a random utility framework and denote the latent utilities of a calendar and a bracelet by $U_{c,i}$ and $U_{b,i}$, respectively.  Because the survey reveals no systematic difference in private valuations for bracelet and calendar across distance (Table \ref{tab:pref-cal-gift}), we model the difference in private value with a single parameter \(\psi\):
\begin{align*}
    U_{c,i} &= \psi + \varepsilon_{c,i} \\
    U_{b,i} &= \varepsilon_{b,i}
\end{align*}

where \(\varepsilon_{c,i}-\varepsilon_{b,i}\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\).  
Hence the probability of initially choosing a calendar is
\begin{align*}
    P(c_{i} = 1) = \Phi(\psi).
\end{align*}
Conditional on that choice, the respondents accepts the cash offer \(m_i\) and switches (\(s_i=1\)) if \(m_i>\psi\). Thus
\begin{align*}
    P(c_{i} = 1, s_{i} = 1) = \Phi(\psi) \times \Phi\left(m_i - \psi \right),
\end{align*}
and analogous expressions hold for the other three outcomes, yielding a two‑stage probit likelihood detailed in Appendix \ref{sec:wtp-model}. 
In the joint model we link the survey‑based preference parameter \(\psi\) to the private benefit coefficients in  \eqref{eq:struct-utility} through 
\begin{align*}
    \beta_{\textrm{bracelet}} &= \beta_{\textrm{Calendar}} + \rho \psi,
\end{align*}
where \(\rho\) maps the \$1 valuation interval into utility units comparable with distance. \\ 

\textbf{Joint Likelihood.} Let $\theta$ denote the full vector of parameters. The joint likelihood can be written as the product of the three submodel likelihoods:
\begin{align} \label{eq:joint-lh}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta | y_i, \textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i, r_i, k_i, c_i, s_i) = \nonumber 
    \\
    \mathcal{L}(\beta_z, \delta, \lambda, \rho, \sigma_u |\  \beta_z^O, \gamma_z^O, \psi&;\  
    y_i, \textrm{treat}_{z,i}, d_i) \times 
    \mathcal{L}(\beta^O_z, \gamma^O_z | r_i, k_i) \times \mathcal{L}(\psi | c_i, s_i)
\end{align}
so that parameter draws must simultaneously rationalize take‑up, observability beliefs, and gift choices.\\

\textbf{Identification.}  
We now outline the sources of identification, working from the preference and observability submodels back to the deworming parameters.

\emph{Private valuation \(\psi\).}  The two‑stage willingness to pay (WTP) experiment in Control communities identifies the difference in purely private utility between the calendar and the bracelet.  Respondents’ initial gift choices, together with their switching decisions at random cash offers, pin down \(\psi\) free of any signaling value.


\emph{Observability parameters \(\beta^{O}_{z}\) and \(\gamma^{O}_{z}\).}  
Holding distance fixed, cross‑incentive arm variation in reported observability identifies \(\beta^{O}_{z}\).  Within an incentive arm, variation across distance identifies \(\gamma^{O}_{z}\).\footnote{Because the full structural model conditions on \(p_{\text{obs}}\), it is invariant to rescaling of \((\beta^{O}_{z},\gamma^{O}_{z})\); we normalize the Control arm to zero.}

\emph{Noise parameter \(\sigma_u\).} 
Variation in take‑up across distance and incentive arms, together with
\(v_i\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) and \(u_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_u^{2})\) independent (so \(w_i\equiv v_i+u_i\sim\mathcal N(0,1+\sigma_u^{2})\)),
identifies \(\sigma_u\).
For the normal mixture, the type inference term is
\[
\Delta(w)
=
\frac{\phi(x)}
     {\sqrt{1+\sigma_u^{2}}\,
      \Phi(x)\,[1-\Phi(x)]},
\qquad
x\equiv \frac{w}{\sqrt{1+\sigma_u^{2}}},
\]
where \(\phi\) and \(\Phi\) denote the standard normal pdf and cdf.\footnote{A derivation of this expression, and an analogous expression with bounded \(v\), appears in Appendix~\ref{delta-deriv}.}
Figure~\ref{fig:harmonized-delta} plots \(\Delta\!\bigl(w^{\ast}(d)\bigr)\) for several values of \(\sigma_u\).
When \(\sigma_u\) is small, \(\Delta(\cdot)\) varies sharply with distance, effectively acting as a non-linear control function in the take‑up probit and facilitating the separation of the social image component from private benefits.
As \(\sigma_u\to\infty\), the noise term dominates \(w\), the density flattens, and \(\Delta(w^{\ast})\) approaches a constant; consequently the social multiplier tends to one, and the social image and private components become observationally equivalent. Under the fixed‑point condition
\(1-\mu'(d)\,\Delta\!\bigl(w^{\ast}(d)\bigr)>0\) \citep{Benabou_laws_2011},
the equilibrium is unique for all finite \(\sigma_u\).

\emph{Remaining parameters.}  
Given \(\psi,\sigma_{u},\beta^{O},\gamma^{O}\), the cost coefficient \(\delta\) is identified by distance‑induced shifts in take‑up, \(\lambda\) by differences in social image returns across observability levels, and the private benefit coefficients by differences in take-up across treatment arms.

% , with an additional restriction on Bracelet and Calendar given by:
% \[
% \beta_{\text{Bracelet}}
% =
% \beta_{\text{Calendar}}
% +\rho\,\psi,
% \]
% where \(\rho\) converts the dollar valued WTP measure into utility units. \\

\textbf{Estimation.}  
Our Bayesian procedure has two steps.  
First, we draw a parameter vector \(\theta\) from its prior, compute the implied objects such as \(p_{\text{Observed}}\) and the WTP parameter \(\psi\), solve the fixed‑point equation in \eqref{eq:w-fp-stat}, and construct the vector of cut‑off types \(w^{\ast}(d)\) for every community distance \(d\). We then evaluate, in turn, the three conditional likelihoods—preference, observability, and deworming—and multiply them to obtain the joint likelihood in \eqref{eq:joint-lh} at that draw. Second, we update $\theta$ by sampling from the joint posterior with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, a gradient‑based Markov‑chain algorithm implemented in Stan \citep{stan}.

Weakly informative priors (Appendix \ref{priors}) regularize the sampler and steer it away from regions where the fixed‑point routine fails to converge, eliminating the need for ad‑hoc box constraints common to numerical optimization. The Bayesian framework also lets us propagate parameter uncertainty directly into the policy exercise of Section \ref{allocation}: at each posterior draw we solve the planner’s allocation problem and thus obtain a full posterior distribution over optimal site allocations.

Before conditioning on the data, we examine the prior predictive distribution: we draw parameter vectors from the priors, simulate implied outcomes (e.g., the social multiplier), and inspect whether these simulations align with economic intuition. Grey bands in subsequent figures display these prior predictive ranges; for instance, our prior over the social multiplier is centered on one (non‑existence) and deliberately diffuse.

\subsection{Structural Results}

\subsubsection{Average Treatment Effects}

\textbf{Deworming Take-up and Observability.}
Tables \ref{tab:beliefs-table} and \ref{tab:struct_overall_effects} report the posterior means from the structural model. These estimates closely track the reduced form estimates in Tables \ref{tab:rf-ates-cts-dist} and \ref{tab:rf-fob-tes-cts-dist}, which use the continuous distance measure.\footnote{Table \ref{tab:struct-model-params} reports posterior means and 95\% credible intervals for the full parameter vector.} 

\emph{Take‑up.}  
Distance reduces participation in the control arm by 15 percentage points, compared to 17.3 points in the reduced form. The bracelet raises take‑up by 7.3 points (reduced form: 8.5). Both Ink and Bracelet have larger effects in Far than in Close communities, with Far–Close differences of 3.9 (95\% CrI 1.3–6.7) and 4.7 (2.4–7.5) points, slightly below the reduced form gaps of 5.6 and 7.6 percentage points. For Calendar the Far–Close difference is essentially zero (0 points, CrI –0.01–0.03), so the bracelet–calendar double difference remains sizable at 3.1 points (reduced form: 4.7).

\emph{Observability.}  
In the control group, distance lowers knowledge of peers’ deworming status by 9 points (reduced form: 12.2). Bracelet increases observability by 9.3 points (95\% CrI 5.4–13.2).  
Ink and Bracelet again yield larger gains in Far than in Close communities—11.5 and 9.9 points, respectively—while the calendar differential is only 3.6 points.  
The bracelet–calendar Far–Close double difference is positive (8 points, CrI 3.5–13.8) and, as in the reduced form (17.3 points), excludes zero.

Overall, the structural estimates are slightly more conservative but more precise than their reduced form counterparts,  reflecting the additional structure imposed by the model. \\ 

\textbf{Social Image Returns versus Private Benefits.}  
Table \ref{tab:signaling_private} decomposes each incentive’s effect into a social image component and a private benefit component via two counterfactual exercises.  
To isolate social image returns we, fix the private utility term at its Control value. To isolate private benefits, we hold the social image term at the Control value.

Bracelet and Ink generate the largest social image returns, raising take‑up by 2.9 percentage points (95\% CrI 1.2–4.9) and 1.8 points (0.4–3.6), respectively, when pooling across distance conditions. Calendar’s social image effect is essentially zero (0.4 points, –0.8–1.7).  The Far–Close differentials in social image returns are 3.3 points (1.2–6.2) for Bracelet and 2.9 points (1.1–5.4) for Ink, versus only 1.1 points (–0.0–2.7) for Calendar, consistent with signaling incentives having greater reputational value at farther distances.

Finally, we estimate that Ink carries a sizable negative private valuation: –4.4 points (–7.3 to –1.5).  This dislike of thumb‑marking, combined with higher travel cost in Far communities, pushes the cut-off type upward; only individuals with very high intrinsic motivation \(v\) deworm when Ink is the signal.\footnote{Although the negative private valuation of ink was not anticipated ex-ante, it is analytically useful: together with distance cost, it shifts the cut-off $w^\ast$ further to the right, strengthening identification of the social image component.} 


\subsubsection{The Social Multiplier} 

We assess whether the negative effect of distance on take-up is mitigated or amplified when social image concerns are taken into account. Figure \ref{fig:sm-decomp} reports the estimated social multiplier, $\tilde{SM}(\text{treat}_{z}, d)$, for each incentive arm—Control, Ink, Bracelet, and Calendar—and its decomposition into type inference and observability components (see the figure notes for details).

\textbf{Mitigation.}
When $\tilde{SM}(\text{treat}_{z},d)<1$, distance has a smaller proportional impact on take-up than under the no-observability benchmark (dashed black line).
For Ink the multiplier is 0.90 at $d=0.75$ km, so a 10\% increase in travel cost yields roughly a 9\% local decline in participation.\footnote{In the probit, the marginal effect is $\partial \bar y / \partial d = f_w(w^\ast)\,\delta\,\tilde{SM}$, so 0.90 is a local proportional effect.}
Bracelet shows a similar pattern: $\tilde{SM}=0.93$ at $d=0$ km, falling to 0.82 at $d=2.5$ km.
Consistent with the Bénabou–Tirole framework, mitigation for Ink is driven mainly by a steeper type inference schedule—i.e., a larger slope of $\Delta(w^\ast)$ (higher $\Delta'$) with little change in observability—whereas for Bracelet an increase in observability $\mu(d)$ accounts for a sizable share of the reduction.


\textbf{Amplification.}
In the Control and Calendar arms, $\tilde{SM}(\mathrm{treat}_z,d)>1$: distance therefore has a larger proportional impact on take‑up than under the no‑observability benchmark. Although the type inference term remains positive ($\Delta'(w^{\ast})>0$), the decline in observability dominates ($\mu'(d)<0$). Evaluated at $d=0$ km, the distance semi‑elasticity is about $1.2\times$ the private cost effect—i.e., a one‑kilometer increase in distance induces roughly a 20\% larger decline in take‑up than in the no-observability benchmark.

In sum, greater distance raises social image returns in the Bracelet and Ink arms, making individuals less sensitive to travel cost; by contrast, declining observability in the Control and Calendar arms makes them more sensitive, thereby amplifying the distance penalty.

\subsubsection{Robustness}

Our main specification measures travel cost by the distance from the community centroid to the treatment site and assumes that adults form expectations on that publicly observable metric. To gauge sensitivity to alternative information sets we estimate three variants (online Appendix \ref{sec:altmodels}).

First, we keep the centroid distance when computing the equilibrium cut-off type but let the private cost in each household’s utility depend on its own household‑to‑site distance.  Second, we adopt a full information model in which every household is assumed to know the exact distance of every other household; algebraically the structure is unchanged, but the fixed point is now solved for all 9,805 adults rather than at the community level.  Third, we replicate the main specification after excluding spatially dispersed clusters—those whose mean squared household distance from the centroid exceeds 0.5$\text{ km}^{2}$—where the centroid may be a poor proxy for individual cost.

Results are reported in Tables \ref{tab:indiv-community-struct}–\ref{tab:nooutlier-struct}.  Across all three variants the estimated effects for Bracelet, Calendar, and the Control arm are essentially unchanged.  The Ink estimates are somewhat less stable: when social image returns are computed at the centroid but costs are household specific, the Ink effect is negative even in Far communities (though still less negative than in Close ones); under full information the Ink effect remains negative and becomes flat across distance.  Dropping dispersed clusters brings the Ink coefficients back in line with the main specification. Taken together, these robustness checks show that the core findings—mitigation for high visibility incentives and amplification for low visibility incentives—are insensitive to alternative cost measures or information sets.

Finally, online Appendix \ref{sec:robust-w} investigates the implications of alternative distributional assumptions for the composite type, $w = v+u$. Simulations with bimodal densities lead to similar qualitative conclusions regarding the social multiplier as the Gaussian, suggesting results are not driven by the normality assumption.


\section{Spatial Allocation with a Social Multiplier}\label{allocation}

We now show how the estimated structural parameters, together with geocoded data on communities and potential points of treatment (PoTs), can guide a policymaker’s spatial allocation decisions.  In particular, we ask how awareness of the social multiplier changes (i) the minimum number of PoTs needed to reach a coverage target and (ii) the locations of those PoTs.\footnote{See Section~\ref{sec:prereg} and Appendix Table~\ref{tab:pap_mapping} for the preregistration summary and mapping. This allocation exercise was not prespecified and is presented as a theory driven extension that applies the estimated parameters to a feasible siting problem.}

\subsection{Policymaker’s Optimization Problem}

We model a policymaker who minimizes the number of funded PoTs subject to attaining an expected deworming rate at least as high as the experimental control mean, \(\overline T^{\text{exp}}_{\text{control}}\).\footnote{Formally,
\(\overline T^{\text{exp}}_{\text{control}}
   = E_{\Theta}\!\bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}
      T(\text{control},d_{ij};\theta)\,
      x_{ij}^{\text{experiment}}\bigr],\)
the posterior expectation of take‑up implied by the experiment’s distance assignments \((x_{ij}^{\text{experiment}})\), with the expectation taken over parameter draws \(\theta\sim\Theta\).}

With prevalence data unavailable—and village density fairly homogeneous (78 percent of communities lie within one standard deviation of the mean spatial density, 95 percent within two; Table \ref{tab:density-community})—the policymaker adopts a uniform coverage target.\footnote{If prevalence data were available, the policymaker could weight communities by disease burden.}  Because policymakers typically fix such a target, the “fewest‑PoTs‑for‑target‑coverage’’ formulation both mirrors practice and avoids specifying a PoT production function; any resources saved by not opening additional sites can be diverted to other health programs.  Maximizing coverage for a fixed PoT budget would coincide with this solution only under additional regularity conditions—an analogue to strong duality in linear or convex optimization—but those conditions fail for the nonlinear take‑up function we estimate.  We therefore adopt the minimization problem as the benchmark.

There are \(n=144\) communities (\(i\)) and \(m=1{,}451\) candidate PoTs (\(j\)).  
Let \(x_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether community \(i\) is served by PoT \(j\) and \(y_j\in\{0,1\}\) whether PoT \(j\) is funded.  
For a given parameter draw \(\theta\) the planner solves the integer program
\begin{align}
\min_{x,y}\;&\sum_{j=1}^{m} y_j  \nonumber \\[2pt]
\text{s.t.}\;&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}
     T(\text{treat}_z,d_{ij};\theta)\,x_{ij}
     \;\ge\;
     \overline T^{\text{exp}}_{\text{control}}, \nonumber\\[2pt]
&\sum_{j=1}^{m} x_{ij}=1,\quad
  x_{ij}\le y_j,\quad
  x_{ij}d_{ij}\le D,\qquad\forall i,j. \label{eq:oa-problem}
\end{align}
Here $T(\textrm{treat}_z, d_{ij};\theta)$ is the expected take-up under treatment $z$ at community-PoT distance \(d_{ij}\) for parameter draw $\theta$. We set \(D=3.5\) km to cap total travel distance.\footnote{The cap balances two goals: (i) limiting extrapolation beyond the 2.5 km range observed in the experiment, and (ii) allowing enough scope to pool nearby villages under a single PoT.  Extending \(D\) to 10 km changes results negligibly (Appendix Table \ref{tab:optim-post-robust}); the posterior assigns virtually no density to positive demand beyond 5 km.}  
The target \(\overline T^{\text{exp}}_{\text{control}}\) is the expected coverage that would arise under the experiment’s distance assignments if every village were in the Control arm:  

\[
\overline T^{\text{exp}}_{\text{control}}
   = E_{\Theta}\!\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}
      T(\text{control},d_{ij};\theta)\,
      x_{ij}^{\text{experiment}}\Bigr].
\]

Because \(\theta\) is treated as a random variable, we solve problem \eqref{eq:oa-problem} for 200 posterior draws and aggregate the resulting solutions to obtain the policymaker’s posterior distribution of the optimal PoT count \(\gamma(\theta)\). Solving at every draw propagates estimation uncertainty that a single plug‑in estimate would understate.


\hypertarget{counterfactual-simulations}{%
\subsubsection{Counterfactual
Simulations}\label{counterfactual-simulations}}
We decompose expected take-up into counterfactual components by holding either 
the private benefit or social image index fixed at Control values. For exposition, 
we label these contributions `private benefit', $B(\cdot)$, and 
`social image return', $\mu\Delta$:
% The structural model decomposes expected take‑up at distance \(d_{ij}\) into a private component \(B(\cdot)\) and a social image component \(\mu\,\Delta\):

\[
T(\text{treat}_z,d_{ij};\theta)
=
\underbrace{B(\text{treat}_z,d_{ij})}_{\text{private benefit}}
+
\underbrace{\mu(\text{treat}_z,d_{ij})\,
            \Delta\bigl(w^{\ast}(\text{treat}_z,d_{ij})\bigr)}
            _{\text{social image return}}.
\]

Holding the private benefit fixed, we vary the social image term to assess how alternative signaling environments affect the number—and spatial pattern—of PoTs required to achieve the coverage target. 

\subsubsection*{Knowledge of the Social Multiplier}

Figure \ref{fig:demand-curve-optim} contrasts the demand curves a policymaker would perceive under different beliefs about the interplay between distance and social image returns. Table \ref{tab:optim-post} reports the corresponding numbers of PoTs. 

We first consider a policymaker who recognizes that social norms matter (\(\mu>0\)) but—having calibrated demand only at short distance (0.5 km)—assumes that social image returns are constant in \(d\). The resulting demand curve (blue line) leads her to allocate 100 PoTs in the Control scenario (Panel B, row 1). When observability is raised with bracelets, she would open 96 PoTs (row 2).

The yellow and red lines correct this misspecification by incorporating the endogenous change in image returns.  Under Control observability (yellow), observability declines with distance, demand falls more steeply, and amplification sets in; the policymaker now needs 107 PoTs, 7\% more than under the constant returns belief (row 3).  Under Bracelet observability (red), image returns rise with distance, flattening the demand curve (mitigation); the required number of PoTs drops to 90, about 6\% fewer than in the constant‑returns case (row 4).  Recognizing the social multiplier therefore shifts resources toward settings where social image concerns render demand more inelastic.

 \subsubsection*{Need for Experimentation at Scale}

Finally, consider a policymaker who is unaware of the social multiplier and therefore sets $\mu=0$. This situation mirrors a pilot that distributes travel vouchers privately, revealing only how take‑up falls with travel distance. Because the incentive is not publicly observable, the pilot cannot shift community beliefs or generate the equilibrium feedback captured by the social multiplier and would only reveal private cost sensitivity. Relying on such data, the policymaker would view demand as highly elastic: even if all 144 candidate PoTs were opened and each community were assigned to its nearest site (average distance 0.58 km), projected take‑up is only 17.4 percent (Table \ref{tab:optim-post}, Panel B, row 5). In practice, once observability and social inferences are accounted for, coverage can be substantially higher with fewer sites.  

These results underscore that identifying—and ultimately leveraging—social image motives requires experimentation at scale, with incentives made sufficiently public to shift equilibrium beliefs.


\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion} \label{conclusion}

Leveraging social image concerns is receiving increasing attention among policymakers as a strategy to enhance the provision of public goods and promote socially desirable behaviors. This study demonstrates that economic incentives do not operate in isolation: private costs and social image concerns interact in equilibrium, with implications for policy design.

We draw three lessons. First, a routinely used non-monetary lever, distance, interacts with social image in ways that materially change demand for preventive care. As expected, moving sites farther reduces take-up, but when actions are publicly observable the associated image payoff can compensate for extra walking. In practice, higher observability softens the travel cost penalty, whereas lower observability amplifies it.

Second, observability can be directly shaped by policy. Simple, low-cost signals make take-up more visible, especially where baseline take-up is low, and can flip the social multiplier from amplification (distance further depresses demand) to mitigation (distance’s negative effect is dampened). In our setting, increasing the observability of actions broadened awareness of others’ take-up and attenuated the sensitivity of take-up to travel distance.

Third, recognizing these interactions reshapes optimal spatial design. A policymaker who calibrates demand at short range and treats image benefits as distance-invariant will misallocate resources. Accounting for the social multiplier shows that public observability can substitute for proximity: with public signals, the same coverage can be achieved with fewer, more widely spaced sites.

Several open questions remain. First, our data capture only short-run responses: whether social image multipliers persist, fade, or grow as communities learn and the novelty of the signal dissipates is unknown. Second, larger experiments that randomize distances across a greater number of clusters would permit non-parametric estimation of the multiplier, relaxing the functional form assumptions we impose here. Third, a full welfare analysis would weigh cost savings against additional travel time and any stigma borne by non-participants. Future work should quantify these distributional implications.

Taken together, the results constitute a proof of concept: while broader external validity remains to be established, they highlight that overlooking how private costs reshape social image incentives can lead to misallocation and leave untapped opportunities for using public observability to expand program reach at lower fiscal cost.

\subsection{Baseline Sample Balance, Demographics, and Worm Knowledge}\label{app:baseline-sample}


\subsubsection{Baseline Balance Sample}
Table~\ref{tab:baseline-bal} reports balance tests for the 1,995 respondents in the baseline sample across demographic characteristics (Panel~A), deworming knowledge (Panel~B), and social image concerns (Panel~C). Each variable is tested using pairwise difference-in-means relative to the control group, as well as joint $F$-tests across all arms, separately for the close, far, and combined  distance arms. At conventional significance thresholds ($p < 0.05$), there are six statistically significant pairwise imbalances out of 128 tests conducted and three significant overall joint tests out of 16 total. This is roughly consistent with the number expected by chance under the null of successful randomization.

\subsubsection{Demographics}
Panel~A of Table~\ref{tab:baseline-bal} shows that the sample is predominantly Christian (97.4\%), with a majority identifying with the Luhya ethnic group (62.3\%). Nearly half of respondents completed primary schooling (47.6\%), and 19.5\% have a floor made of tile or cement, indicating that a substantial share of the sample lives in modest material conditions. Demographic characteristics are well balanced across treatment arms: no pairwise comparison reaches statistical significance in either the close or far stratum, and all joint $F$-tests are far from conventional thresholds.

\subsubsection{Deworming Knowledge}
Panel~B of Table~\ref{tab:baseline-bal} shows that baseline deworming knowledge is high. Nearly all respondents know that children can get worms (93.4\%), and a large majority know the same is true for adults (69.9\%). Knowledge that medication treats worms is widespread at 78.2\%, and very few (1.9\%) hold the misconception that deworming is only for the sick. Prior deworming experience is also common: 68.4\% report having ever been dewormed, and 37.4\% were dewormed in the past year. Awareness of the recommended bi-yearly treatment schedule stands at 47.0\%, and 37.1\% are aware that worm infections impose externalities on others. Overall, the sample display a solid foundation of deworming knowledge and prior engagement with deworming.

\subsubsection{Social Image Concerns}
Panel~C of Table~\ref{tab:baseline-bal} reports on baseline social norms and image concerns. We find that health behaviors such as deworming and child immunization generate strong positive social image: Nearly all respondents (\text{94.7\%}) report they would praise an individual for deworming, and \text{95.9\%} would praise someone for immunizing a child. Similarly, the majority of respondents believe that failing to invest in these health behaviors would lead to negative social judgment: \text{69.9\%} state they would judge someone for not deworming, and \text{77.9\%} would judge someone for failing to immunize a child.

% ── Baseline sample ───────────────────────────────────────────────
Figure \ref{fig:sample-consort} displays how each survey maps onto our main analysis samples:

\emph{Baseline survey.} The baseline sampling frame was drawn from the household
census roster of 39{,}301 adults across 144 clusters.  From this frame, 4{,}185
individuals were selected by randomly drawing 15 households per cluster (additionally, we drew two backup 
households per cluster).  Enumerators attempted to reach a
target of 2{,}160 individuals; of the 2{,}062 successfully located, 38 were
unable to participate (e.g. due to illness or language barriers) and 29
refused consent, yielding 1{,}995 completed baseline interviews that
constitute the ``Baseline sample''.

The full 4{,}185-person baseline roster plays a second role beyond the baseline
survey itself: it was added to the administrative monitoring lists used to
record deworming take-up at points of treatment on the day of the campaign.  As
a result, the baseline roster contributes approximately one-fifth of the broader
monitored take-up sample, discussed in the next section.

% ── Endline sample ────────────────────────────────────────────────

\emph{Endline survey.} The endline sampling roster consisted of 11{,}166
individuals drawn from the household census roster.  From this roster, 4{,}220
individuals were selected, comprising a main roster of approximately 25 per
cluster plus roughly two backup individuals per cluster, and enumerators
successfully located 3{,}830 of them.  Of those reached, 78 were unable to
participate and 23 refused consent, yielding 3{,}729 completed endline
interviews.  The PAP pre-specified 3{,}600 completions (25 per cluster $\times$
144 clusters), the overshoot is driven by the (mistaken) systematic surveying of
all backup individuals. Excluding the SMS treatment arm, which is set aside for
the SMS analysis, the ``Endline sample'' used for the main analysis comprises
2{,}659 respondents.  The full 11{,}166-person endline roster is added to the
administrative monitoring lists for their deworming status to be recorded at
points of treatment during the campaign.


The endline survey generates three further subsamples used throughout the
analysis.  First, a random half of endline respondents were assigned to an
observability module eliciting beliefs about peers' deworming take-up (1{,}784
respondents), of whom 1{,}141 remain after excluding the SMS treatment arm,
the ``Endline observability sample''. There are 255 individuals with
missing observability module data due to a programming error, discussed in Section \ref{sec:sample-attrit}.  Second, all endline respondents stated a preference between a
bracelet and a calendar as a thank-you gift (\emph{gift choice}).  Our analysis 
using this gift-choice data focuses on individuals that did not choose to deworm
in the control group (1{,}350 individuals). Third, we conducted a
willingness-to-pay (WTP) exercise for the bracelet gift in the control clusters,
which is used to estimate a WTP model for the bracelet.  In this WTP exercise,
we attempted to reach 600 individuals across 34 control clusters, of whom we
successfully reached 474 and obtained completed WTP data from 467, ``WTP sample''.


Because the endline roster is drawn randomly from the household census, endline
respondents partly overlap with the baseline sample, but we are unable to link
baseline and endline records at the household level.  The two surveys therefore
provide independent cross-sectional snapshots of the same communities rather
than a panel.

% ── Take-up analysis sample ───────────────────────────────────────

\emph{Take-up analysis sample.}
Deworming take-up was recorded administratively at points of treatment for all
individuals on the monitoring lists, which were assembled from the baseline
roster (4{,}185 individuals) and the endline roster (11{,}166 individuals). Unfortunately, 
a SurveyCTO coding error caused 989 individuals from the endline roster to be omitted from the 
administrative monitoring lists, and thus they are absent from the take-up analysis sample, further discussion including differential attrition tests can be found in Section \ref{sec:sample-attrit}.
Because the two rosters were drawn independently from the same census, some
individuals appear on both lists; after deduplication the combined monitoring
universe comprises 12{,}827 unique individuals.
Excluding the SMS treatment arm, which was assigned to receive social
information or reminder messages and is reserved for the SMS analysis, yields
the take-up analysis sample of 9{,}805 individuals used throughout the
main results.

  

\subsection{Perceived meaning and negative perceptions of visibility incentives (sms.control)}\label{app:bad_incentive}

This appendix provides descriptive evidence on (i) what the visibility incentives were perceived to signal and (ii) whether respondents expressed any negative associations with the incentives. The sample is restricted to endline respondents assigned to \texttt{sms.treatment==``sms.control''} and to the incentive arms (Calendar/Ink/Bracelet).

\paragraph{Perceived meaning and stigma.}
Respondents were asked whether they had seen their assigned incentive in the community, and (conditional on reporting having seen it) an open-ended question about what it means if someone has that incentive (\texttt{cal\_meaning}, \texttt{ink\_meaning}, \texttt{bracelet\_meaning}). Among respondents who report seeing the incentive and provided a non-missing meaning response ($N=1{,}669$), the overwhelming majority interpret the incentives as indicating deworming participation/treatment: 96.4\% in the Ink arm, 93.5\% in the Bracelet arm, and 88.5\% in the Calendar arm mention deworming/treatment/medicine in their response. Explicitly stigmatizing interpretations are essentially absent: none of the 1,669 meaning responses contain terms suggesting that the person is dirty/unclean or infected (e.g., \emph{``dirty/chafu''} or \emph{``infection/ugonjwa''}). A very small share (0.2--0.4\% depending on arm) mention worms without explicit treatment language, typically framed as prevention or being free of worms (e.g., \emph{``Those who have it are free from worms''}). An illustrative deworming-linked response is: \emph{``It shows the person went for deworming treatment.''}

\paragraph{Incidence of negative perceptions.}
To assess whether the visibility devices generated stigma or discomfort, endline respondents were asked whether there was anything they did not like about the assigned incentive (\texttt{bad\_bracelet}, \texttt{bad\_ink}, \texttt{bad\_calendar}). Respondents answering ``yes'' were asked an open-ended follow-up explaining what they did not like (\texttt{bad\_incentive}). These ``bad'' items were collected only for Wave 2 respondents, which is why the number asked is smaller than the full endline sample. Table~\ref{tab:bad_incentive_smscontrol} reports the share of respondents reporting any drawback by incentive arm. Complaints are uncommon overall (Ink mean about 3.3\%), and complaint rates for Bracelet are statistically indistinguishable from Ink. Calendar recipients are more likely to report some drawback (about 5.5 percentage points higher than Ink), but the open-ended explanations indicate that these concerns primarily reflect design or informational preferences rather than stigma.

\paragraph{Content of open-ended complaints.}
Table~\ref{tab:bad_breakdown_combined} codes the open-ended \texttt{bad\_incentive} responses into salient themes. A key pattern for the calendar is that, among the small set of complainers, the modal complaint is that the calendar should more clearly state who provided it and that it is linked to deworming (Panel A: 20 of 31 complainers). Respondents explicitly asked that the calendar include the NGO name/logo and/or text or imagery indicating the deworming campaign. This pattern is inconsistent with respondents viewing deworming as something to hide; instead, it suggests that a subset of respondents preferred the calendar to more clearly communicate the campaign purpose.

For ink, complaints almost exclusively concern the mark being dirty/sticky or slow to fade (Panel B). For bracelets, the most common complaints relate to religious objections, fit/comfort, and appearance (Panel C). Explicitly stigma-related comments are very rare in this module (one bracelet complainer mentioned prostitution and one calendar complainer referenced a family-planning rumor).

\subsection{PoT Selection and Distance Randomization}\label{sec:site-selection}

This appendix provides implementation details on (i) the geographically constrained selection of PoT-centered clusters
and (ii) the Close/Far distance assignment and targeted community selection. The notation used matches that used in the Cluster Selection Algorithm section 4.4 of the pre-analysis plan.

\paragraph{Definitions.}
A point of treatment (PoT) is the location at which CHVs delivered deworming during the campaign.
We use geo-coded primary schools and religious centers as spatial reference points: these locations provide feasible centralized sites for PoTs
and, because schools/centers are dense in the study area, a proxy for the location of nearby communities in the absence of geo-coded community boundaries. A cluster is defined by a PoT together with its surrounding 2.5~km catchment radius. This 2.5~km catchment delimits the set of communities eligible to be selected as the targeted community for that PoT.

\paragraph{Sampling frame.}
We begin from a sampling frame of 1{,}451 geo-coded primary schools/religious centers across the three study counties that could serve
as candidate PoT locations.\footnote{Coordinates are from the Kenya Open Data Portal: \url{http://www.opendata.go.ke/}.}
Each school/center defines a candidate cluster with a 2.5~km catchment radius. We selected 158 clusters (150 targets plus
8 fallbacks) and field feasibility constraints limited implementation to 144 clusters.%%YOU NEED TO BE MORE SPECIFIC 

\subsubsection*{PoT selection under spatial overlap constraints}

We select clusters using a sequential acceptance--rejection algorithm. Let \(C\) denote the set of all candidate clusters
(\(|C|=1{,}451\)) and let \(R\) denote the set of accepted clusters. The algorithm iterates through candidates drawn
at random, adding a candidate cluster to \(R\) only if doing so satisfies a minimum non-overlapping catchment size
requirement for all clusters in \(R\). If the algorithm cannot reach the target number of clusters under the
constraints, the procedure restarts from the full candidate set.

\paragraph{Non-overlapping catchment size (ClusterSize).}
For a candidate cluster \(i\) and an accepted set \(R\), define the non-overlapping catchment of \(i\) as the
portion of \(i\)'s 2.5~km catchment circle that does not lie within the (larger) catchment areas used to buffer
already selected PoTs (described below). We operationalize the size of this non-overlapping catchment by counting
``available'' primary school/religous center neighborhoods. Specifically, a location counts as available if at least 60\% of a 1~km radius
circle around the center lies within the non-overlapping portion of the 2.5~km catchment. We require each selected cluster to retain at least one such
available school/center neighborhood (i.e., \(\text{ThresholdClusterSize}=1\)). Intuitively, this ensures that overlap
restrictions do not leave a cluster with an effectively empty area from which to list eligible communities.

\paragraph{Buffers used for overlap checks.}
Although the 2.5~km radius defines the eligibility region for selecting study communities, overlap restrictions are
evaluated using larger catchment areas to create buffers between nearby PoTs. To fit the study area, candidate PoTs are randomly assigned a larger catchment radius of either 3~km or 4~km. Figure~\ref{fig:cluster-overlap-schematic} illustrates this geometry: the shaded region represents the portion of a catchment that is excluded because it falls within a neighbor's buffer. Study communities are selected from the remaining unshaded portion of the 2.5~km catchment. As a result, any selected study community is at least 0.5~km closer to its own PoT than to the nearest other selected PoT.

\subsubsection*{Close/Far assignment}

After PoT selection, clusters are randomly assigned, stratified by county, to Close or Far. The distance category is defined by the distance from the selected community centroid to its assigned PoT: 0--1.25~km in Close clusters and 1.25--2.5~km in Far clusters. This cutoff is depicted as the dotted circle in Figure~\ref{fig:cluster-overlap-schematic}, separating the inner (Close) and outer (Far) bands.%%ADDRESS IMBALANCE HERE

\subsubsection*{Targeted community selection}
Within each selected cluster, we select an anchor school/religious center as a field reference point for listing nearby communities. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cluster-overlap-schematic}, the anchor point is chosen to ensure that the randomly selected target community lies in the cluster's assigned distance band. In Close clusters, the anchor is typically the PoT. In Far clusters, the anchor is chosen in the outer ring so that listed communities lie 1.25--2.5~km from the PoT.

Enumerators list all eligible communities near the anchor and we randomly select one as the study's targeted community. If the initially selected community was too small for surveying, an additional nearby community was included. In all but two clusters, the assigned PoT is the closest deworming site to the selected community. Because PoT and community locations were finalized after community enumeration, randomized re-assignment occurred in six communities due to logistical constraints in realized PoT locations.

\subsubsection*{Potential distance balance under constrained PoT selection}

A potential concern is that the acceptance-rejection algorithm may have introduced systematic differences in treatment probabilities, violating the assumption of random assignment. Following \citet{borusyak2023nonrandom}, we assess this by simulating the realized selection and assignment mechanism: we re-run the full algorithm 100 times and compute, for each study community, the implied distribution of centroid-to-PoT distances under these counterfactual re-draws. We implement this simulation after data collection, so the set of surveyed communities is fixed. We therefore compute potential distances using counterfactually selected PoTs and the already surveyed community centroids. We have broad geographic coverage, and each counterfactual PoT has at least one eligible surveyed community to map to under the simulated assignment. Figure~\ref{fig:bh-dens-plot} shows that the implied potential distance distributions are statistically indistinguishable for communities assigned Close versus Far.

\subsection{Experimenter demand and social desirability checks}
\label{app:demand_checks}

\subsubsection*{Motivation}
A potential concern with survey elicited beliefs about peers' deworming participation is experimenter demand or social desirability. Respondents may feel pressure to (i) provide a definite answer rather than responding ``Don't know,'' or (ii) overstate peers' participation (e.g., reporting that non-participants came for deworming treatment).

\subsubsection*{Two elicitation formats}
To probe these concerns, endline respondents were randomized into one of two formats for the ``knowledge of others deworming participation'' module.%CHECK THIS IS CONSISTENT WORDING WITH WHAT USE CURRENTLY IN MAIN TEXT 

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Table A: named individuals.} Respondents were asked about ten named individuals one-by-one. For each name, respondents first indicated whether they recognized the person (Yes/No). If recognized, they then reported whether they thought the person came for deworming (Yes/No/Don't know).
  \item \textbf{Table B: anonymized pairs.} Respondents were shown ten pairs of names and first reported how many of the two they recognized (0/1/2), without indicating which name(s) they recognized. The pair format is designed to reduce privacy concerns and the perceived sensitivity of revealing knowledge about specific individuals. When exactly one name in the pair was recognized, respondents were asked whether the person they knew came for deworming (Yes/No/Don't know), again without indicating which person in the pair they knew. When both names were recognized, respondents were additionally asked whether both people in the pair came for treatment. Because this latter question refers to a joint event rather than an individual's take-up, we do not use it in the comparisons below. We focus instead on instances when respondents recognized one person in the pair, which is the closest analogue to Table A. 
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection*{Sample} We restrict the sample to respondents who recognize at least one person/pair in the module, so that the respondent is engaged with the task. \subsubsection*{Empirical checks}
We conduct three complementary checks.

\paragraph{(1) Recognition reporting.}
Because Table B presents two names at once, we compare recognition per name rather than the probability of recognizing at least one name. Specifically, recognition per name is coded 0/1 for Table A and 0/0.5/1 for Table B.

\paragraph{(2) Experimenter demand effects: Don't know responses.}
To test whether the named individual format induces demand to provide a definite answer, we compare the share of ``Don't know'' responses among recognized individuals. We compare Table A answers for recognized individuals to Table B answers to the ``person you knew'' question, which is asked when exactly one name is recognized in the pair.

\paragraph{(3) Social desirability: misclassification relative to administrative truth.}
Using administrative records for whether the referenced individual actually dewormed, we compare false positives and false negatives. In Table A, truth is observed directly for each named individual. In Table B, because the survey does not record which of the two names was recognized, we score truth for the ``person you knew'' question only when both individuals in the pair share the same administrative status. Specifically, we use pairs where both individuals are recoded as not having come for treatment (both 0) to compute the false positive rate $P(\text{Yes}\mid\text{Truth}=0)$, and pairs where both individuals are recorded as having dewormed (both 1) to compute the false negative rate $P(\text{No}\mid\text{Truth}=1)$. This restriction is conservative and ensures that the administrative status of the ``known person'' is identified.

\subsubsection*{Results}
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:demand_checks} summarizes the evidence. Table B increases reported recognition per name by 11.4 percentage points (from 47.8 to 59.3, $p <0.001$), consistent with reduced privacy pressure in the anonymized pair format. However, we find no evidence that the named individual format induces demand to provide definite answers: the ``Don't know’’ share is 20.4 percent in Table A and 19.7 percent in Table B (B--A = -0.7, $p=0.610$). We also find no statistically significant increase in false positives under Table A: the false positive rate $P(\text{Yes}\mid\text{Truth}=0)$ is slightly lower in Table B than in Table A (B--A = -3.3, $p=0.140$). Finally, Table B exhibits higher false negative rates among true participants (B--A = 5.6, $p=0.005$). Overall, while recognition reporting is format-sensitive, the evidence does not suggest that Table A inflates reports of peers' participation.

A remaining concern is that respondents might strategically answer ``I don't recognize this person'' in Table A to avoid stating that a known non-participant did not come for treatment. While we cannot observe latent recognition directly, we can test whether administrative take-up predicts reported recognition differently across formats.

In Table A, among dyads with observed administrative truth, the recognition rate is 52.5 percent when the referenced individual truly dewormed and 43.4 percent when the referenced individual did not (difference 9.1 pp). In Table B, we can link truth to recognition only in the subset of pairs where both individuals share the same administrative status. In that subset, recognition per name is 64.4 percent in pairs where both individuals dewormed and 54.8 percent in pairs where both did not (difference 9.6 pp). Importantly, the truth--recognition gradient is not statistically different across formats: a difference-in-differences regression yields an interaction estimate of 0.5 pp (SE 0.016; $p=0.754$). Moreover, Table B increases recognition by roughly the same amount for true non-participants and true participants (Table B -- Table A differences of 11.4 and 11.9, respectively). Together, these patterns suggest that format-driven differences in recognition reporting are not disproportionately concentrated among true non-participants,  making it less likely that strategic under-reporting of recognition in Table A is driving the main observability results.

\subsection{Ruling Out Pure Private Utility Substitution}
\label{sec:mrs}

The reduced form evidence produces two key facts:  
(i) signal incentives (Bracelet, Ink) increase take‑up more in Far than in Close communities;  
(ii) the willingness to pay (WTP) exercise shows no distance‑related difference in the private valuation of a bracelet relative to a calendar,  
\[
\mathbb E\!\bigl[v_{\mathrm B}-v_{\mathrm C}\mid d\bigr]=0
\quad\text{for all } d,
\tag{F.1}
\]
where \(v_{\mathrm B},v_{\mathrm C}\) denote respondents’ monetary valuations of the two items.  
This appendix shows that a binary choice model driven only by private utility and distance cost cannot fit both facts simultaneously.

\paragraph{Latent utility model.}
Let \(B_i,C_i\in\{0,1\}\) indicate assignment to the Bracelet or Calendar arm (Control: \(B_i=C_i=0\)), and let \(d_i\) be the community–to–PoT distance. Define \(y_i^{*}\) as the latent net utility of deworming for individual \(i\); we assume
\begin{equation}
  y_i^{*}= \beta_0
           +\beta_{\mathrm B} B_i
           +\beta_{\mathrm C} C_i
           +\delta\,d_i
           +\gamma_{\mathrm B}(B_i d_i)
           +\gamma_{\mathrm C}(C_i d_i)
           +\varepsilon_i,\quad
           \varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,1),
  \label{eq:latent}
\end{equation}
and set the observed decision \(y_i=\mathbf 1\{y_i^{*}>0\}\).

\paragraph{Relative marginal utilities.}
The ratio of marginal private utilities of a bracelet versus a calendar is
\begin{equation}
  R(d)=
  \frac{\partial y_i^{*}/\partial B_i}{\partial y_i^{*}/\partial C_i}
  =
  \frac{\beta_{\mathrm B}+\gamma_{\mathrm B} d}
       {\beta_{\mathrm C}+\gamma_{\mathrm C} d}.
  \label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:mrs}
  Suppose (F.1) holds.  
  Then model \eqref{eq:latent} can replicate the larger Bracelet effect at long distances only if the interaction coefficients satisfy
  \[
     \gamma_{\mathrm B}\beta_{\mathrm C}-\gamma_{\mathrm C}\beta_{\mathrm B}\neq 0,
  \tag{F.2}
  \]
  which contradicts (F.1). Therefore a model with private utility and distance cost alone cannot explain both empirical facts.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Differentiating \eqref{eq:ratio} with respect to \(d\) yields
\[
  R'(d)
  =\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm B}\beta_{\mathrm C}-\gamma_{\mathrm C}\beta_{\mathrm B}}
        {(\beta_{\mathrm C}+\gamma_{\mathrm C}d)^{2}}.
\]
Hence \(R(d)\) is constant in \(d\) iff \(\gamma_{\mathrm B}\beta_{\mathrm C}-\gamma_{\mathrm C}\beta_{\mathrm B}=0\).  
This equality obtains only in two cases:

\smallskip
\noindent
(i) \(\gamma_{\mathrm B}=\gamma_{\mathrm C}=0\), implying no distance interaction;  
(ii) exact proportionality, \(\gamma_{\mathrm B}/\beta_{\mathrm B}=\gamma_{\mathrm C}/\beta_{\mathrm C}\neq 0\).

\smallskip
Empirically, the signal-distance interaction is positive and significant (\(\gamma_{\mathrm B}>0\)), whereas the Calendar interaction is indistinguishable from zero (\(\gamma_{\mathrm C}\approx 0\)).  Neither case (i) nor case (ii) holds, so \(R(d)\) must vary with \(d\), contradicting the distance‑invariant valuation gap (F.1).
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Empirical check.}
Table \ref{tab:pref-cal-gift} confirms the premise (F.1): the probability that a respondent chooses the bracelet over the calendar when offered a free gift is the same in Close and Far communities ($p=0.40$ and $p=0.49$, respectively).  Consequently, heterogeneous substitution in private utility cannot by itself account for the stronger signaling incentive effects observed at greater distances; an additional distance modulated component—such as social image utility—is required.

\subsection{WTP Likelihood}\label{sec:wtp-model}

Let each individual \(i\) have a latent valuation
\[
  g_i^{\text{wtp}}\sim N\!\bigl(\psi,\,1\bigr),
\]
where \(\psi\) (to be estimated) is the mean difference in monetary value between calendar and bracelet and the variance is fixed at one for scale identification.

\paragraph{Stage 1 (item choice).}
Respondent \(i\) first chooses between a calendar and a bracelet.  Define
\[
  c_i =
  \begin{cases}
    1, & \text{calendar chosen},\\
    0, & \text{bracelet chosen},
  \end{cases}
\qquad
c_i=1 \Longleftrightarrow\; g_i^{\text{wtp}}>0 .
\]
Hence \(\psi>0\) means that on average individuals tend to prefer the calendar.

\paragraph{Stage 2 (cash offer and switch decision).}
After the initial choice, a random cash offer \(m_i\) (in KSh) is drawn.  The respondent may switch to the other item.  Let
\[
  s_i =
  \begin{cases}
    1, & \text{switch occurs},\\
    0, & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
\]

\begin{itemize}
  \item If \(c_i=0\), switching to the calendar occurs when \(-m_i < g_i^{\text{wtp}}<0\).
  \item If \(c_i=1\), switching to the bracelet occurs when \(0< g_i^{\text{wtp}}< m_i\).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Likelihood.}
With \(\Phi(\cdot)\) denoting the standard normal c.d.f., the individual contribution to the likelihood is
\[
\begin{aligned}
L_i(\psi)=\;
&\bigl[\Phi(-m_i-\psi)\bigr]^{\mathbf 1\{c_i=0,s_i=0\}}
\;\times\;
\bigl[1-\Phi(m_i-\psi)\bigr]^{\mathbf 1\{c_i=1,s_i=0\}} \\
&\times\;
\bigl[\Phi(-\psi)-\Phi(-m_i-\psi)\bigr]^{\mathbf 1\{c_i=0,s_i=1\}}
\;\times\;
\bigl[\Phi(m_i-\psi)-\Phi(-\psi)\bigr]^{\mathbf 1\{c_i=1,s_i=1\}} .
\end{aligned}
\]

Posterior summaries of \(\psi\) are reported in Table \ref{tab:wtp-summ-table}.

\subsection{Priors}\label{priors}

We estimate the structural model (Section~\ref{structural}) in a Bayesian framework and therefore have to specify the distributions from which we draw parameters

\[
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{deworming}}
    &= \bigl\{\,
        \beta_{\text{control}} \sim N(0,1),\;
        \beta_{z} \sim N(0,0.25),\;
        \delta \sim N(0,0.25),\\
    &\qquad\lambda \sim N^{+}(0,0.25),\;
        \sigma_{u} \sim \text{Inv\,Gamma}(3.3,1.1),\;
        \rho \sim N^{+}(0,0.001)
      \bigr\},\\[6pt]
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{wtp}}
    &= \{\,
        \psi \sim N(0,2)
      \},\\[6pt]
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{observability}}
    &= \{\,
        \beta^{O}_{z},\,\gamma^{O}_{z} \sim N(0,0.125)
      \}.
\end{aligned}
\]


For the treatment effect parameters (\(\beta\)’s) we adopt weakly informative priors centered at zero, reflecting the view that, absent data, incentives are assumed not to change observability beliefs or confer extra private utility. In other words, we do not rule out social signaling for any of the incentives; instead, we assume ex ante that their effects do not differ.

Two additional parameters are difficult to learn from the data.  We constrain \(\rho\) to be positive and close to zero with a strongly informative prior, reflecting limited prior knowledge about consumption value differences between bracelets and calendars.  Likewise, we place an inverse‑Gamma prior on \(\sigma_{u}\) that keeps this scale parameter away from zero for numerical stability.  Both choices are conservative: larger \(\sigma_{u}\) flattens \(\Delta^{\ast}\), making signaling harder to detect, while a smaller \(\rho\) assigns more of the bracelet effect to private utility rather than signaling.

\subsection{Alternative Structural Models}\label{sec:altmodels}

This appendix estimates three departures from the baseline specification in Section \ref{structural}. Each alternative model preserves the main signaling mechanism—how observability generates social image payoffs—and the baseline distribution of intrinsic types. The only change is in how travel distance enters private utility and/or the social image term.

\subsubsection{Private Distance Costs and Community Social Image Returns}\label{sec:private-cost-community-rep}

In many settings adults know their own walking distance but form beliefs about others using a public reference point such as the community centroid. Let \(d_{ic}\) be household \(i\)’s distance and \(\bar d_{c}\) the centroid distance of community \(c\). The fixed‑point equation therefore uses \(\bar d_{c}\),
\[
  w^{\ast}(z,\bar d_{c})
  \;=\;
  -\!\bigl(z\beta_{z}-\delta \bar d_{c}\bigr)
  -\mu(z,\bar d_{c})\,
   \Delta\!\bigl(w^{\ast}(z,\bar d_{c})\bigr),
\]
while individual \(i\) deworms if
\[
  z\beta_{z}-\delta d_{ic}
  +\mu(z,\bar d_{c})\,
   \Delta\!\bigl(w^{\ast}(z,\bar d_{c})\bigr)
  +w_{i}
  >0.
\]
Substituting \(w^{\ast}(\cdot)\) shows that the deworming probability is
\[
  \Pr(\text{Deworm}_{ic}\mid z,d_{ic},\bar d_{c})
  =F_{w}\!\bigl(-w^{\ast}(z,\bar d_{c})+\delta(\bar d_{c}-d_{ic})\bigr).
\]
That is, an individual’s decision to deworm equals the community cut‑off type plus an offset depending on how much farther (or closer) the household is from the PoT than the centroid. Table \ref{tab:indiv-community-struct} reports posterior average treatment effects: the Bracelet and Calendar estimates are very similar to those in the main specification, whereas Ink becomes negative even in Far communities and its Far-Close becomes smaller and insignificant.

Then both the private cost and the social image return depend on the household specific distance \(d_{ic}\). \subsubsection{Excluding Spatially Dispersed Clusters}\label{sec:no-outliers}

The centroid may mismeasure cost in communities that are geographically spread out. We drop clusters whose mean‑squared household distance from the centroid exceeds \(0.5\text{ km}^{2}\) and re-estimate the main specification. \subsection{Sensitivity to the Distribution of \texorpdfstring{$w$}{w}}\label{sec:robust-w}

The main specification assumes that the composite type \(w=v+u\) is Gaussian.  
This section shows that our qualitative conclusions are robust to departures from normality.

\paragraph{Conditions for a Social Multiplier.}
As in \citet{Benabou_laws_2011}, a social multiplier different from one requires  
(i) non‑zero observability, $\mu(z,d)\neq 0$, and  
(ii) a non‑flat social image schedule, $\Delta'(w)\neq 0$.  
The latter fails, for example, under a uniform type distribution, in which gains in honor are exactly offset by losses in stigma.

\paragraph{A potential concern.}
Suppose instead that $w$ is bimodal—one mass of adults highly motivated to deworm and another strongly opposed—and that the local mix of types happens to covary with distance. Could such heterogeneous private values alone generate the distance–by-signal interactions we observe?

\paragraph{Simulation design.}
We simulate two data‑generating processes: Unimodal: $w\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ (our baseline) and Bimodal: an equally weighted mixture of \(\mathcal N(-\eta,1)\) and \(\mathcal N(+\eta,1)\) with \(\eta\) large enough that the two modes are distinct. For each process we vary the travel cost \(b\) and compute  
(i) the equilibrium participation rate $\bar{y}(b)$,  
(ii) the social image return $\Delta(w^{\ast}(b))$, and  
(iii) the implied social multiplier $M(b)$.

\paragraph{Key findings.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Flat regions occur only under a bimodal type density.}  
  Figure~\ref{fig:H1-dens} contrasts the unimodal (Gaussian) and bimodal mixtures we simulate.  When the cut‑off type moves through the density gap between the two modes, the participation curve $\bar y(b)$ in Figure~\ref{fig:H2-takeup} becomes locally flat.  Over exactly the same distance band the social image return $\Delta(w^{\ast}(b))$ is constant (Figure~\ref{fig:H3-delta}), so the social multiplier collapses to $M(b)=1$ (Figure~\ref{fig:H4-mult}). No such flat segment appears in the empirical distance range, ruling out bimodality driven substitution as the sole explanation for our findings.

  \item \textbf{Any local curvature in the type density is sufficient to generate $M\neq1$.}  With the Gaussian benchmark, the slope of the density is never zero in the experimental window, so changes in distance always shift $\Delta(w^{\ast}(b))$ and hence $M(b)$.  The conclusion is distribution robust: any density that is not locally flat yields amplification or mitigation effects similar to those we estimate.

  \item \textbf{Large unobserved noise would also flatten $M(b)$—but is rejected by the data.}  
  A large idiosyncratic shock $\sigma_u$ would effectively smooth the density even if $v$ were Gaussian, pushing $M(b)\approx1$.  Our posterior places little mass on such high variance values; the estimated $\Delta$ retains marked curvature, consistent with the signal-by-distance interactions in the reduced form.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Implication.}
The simulations show that a social multiplier equals one only when the type density is locally flat—either because the cut‑off moves through the gap between two modes, or because idiosyncratic noise \(\sigma_u\) is so large that the effective density is nearly uniform.  In the data we see neither (i) any distance band with flat take‑up nor (ii) estimates of \(\sigma_u\) in the flattening range.  Hence heterogeneous private utility substitution alone cannot explain the stronger distance interaction for highly observable incentives.\\

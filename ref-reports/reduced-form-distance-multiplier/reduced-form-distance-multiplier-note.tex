\section*{A Reduced-Form Relative Distance Multiplier}

This note asks how much of the paper's multiplier result can be recovered
without the structural latent-type and reporting models.  We use the canonical
no-SMS take-up sample of 9,805 adults in 144 communities.  The primary
comparison pools Control and Calendar as No Signal and Ink and Bracelet as Any
Signal.  All specifications include arm indicators, county fixed effects,
sex, age, and the design-based expected-distance control.  Uncertainty comes
from 1,000 county-stratified exponential community-weight draws.

Table~\ref{tab:rf-distance-multiplier} reports three versions of the distance
response.  The original-assignment column uses the randomized Close/Far
assignment.  The linear column estimates an arm-group-specific slope in
realized community-centroid distance.  The spline column uses a three-degree
natural spline and reports the average finite slope from 0.5 to 2.0 km, an
interval inside the common empirical support of all four arms.

The design-based result is especially clear.  Moving from Close to Far lowers
take-up by 18.8 percentage points in No Signal communities and by 6.7
percentage points in Any Signal communities.  The difference is 12.2
percentage points, with a 95 percent interval of 5.2 to 19.3 percentage
points.  Public signals therefore leave 35 percent of the no-signal distance
response, corresponding to 65 percent attenuation.  The continuous-distance
specifications are more conservative but tell the same qualitative story:
the linear estimate implies 39 percent attenuation and the flexible finite
change implies 44 percent attenuation.

This exercise directly identifies total equilibrium distance responses.  If
the underlying direct access-cost response is common across randomized signal
environments, their ratio identifies the relative multiplier,
\[
  \frac{\partial E[Y\mid d,\text{Any Signal}]/\partial d}
       {\partial E[Y\mid d,\text{No Signal}]/\partial d}
  =
  \frac{M_{\text{Any Signal}}(d)}{M_{\text{No Signal}}(d)}.
\]
It does not identify an absolute multiplier relative to zero observability,
because every experimental arm retains positive observability.  The flexible
derivative difference is also not uniformly separated from zero at every
point in the distance interval.  The defensible reduced-form finding is the
finite-change ordering: public signals substantially flatten the take-up
response to distance.  Absolute arm-specific multiplier levels remain the
additional contribution of the structural model.
